# The translator's own way of working — one-to-one session guide

*For use with any of the nine Mixed Nuts translators. One person, one
hour, one notebook. Adapt freely; this is a menu, not a script.*

---

> **Booked length (2026-09-09).** Nine 45-minute slots are held on Adam's
> calendar across 14–23 September, deliberately split between early
> mornings and late afternoons so that people in East Asia have a workable
> option. The hour-long shape below is the full form; in a 45-minute slot
> the watching stays (it is the point) and the showing shrinks to one
> thing or none. Where the following slot is free, let it run to the hour
> only if the translator offers.

## Purpose

The written questionnaire tells me what people are willing to put in an
email. It cannot show me the twenty minutes before the first English word
appears, the book that never leaves arm's reach, the private mark beside a
word someone means to come back to, or the search that failed because of a
spelling nobody will ever mention. That material only surfaces when one
person walks me through one real passage at their own desk.

So this session has three jobs, and none of them is showing off the tool.

1. **Get their day written down as it is** — what arrives, what they do to
   it, what leaves, and what they redo by hand every single time.
2. **Find the boundaries** — where a tool must never appear, what would
   make them abandon one in a single stroke, and which of their checks are
   judgement (the tool must not touch them) rather than mechanical (the
   tool may).
3. **Convert their distrust into a specification.** Whoever I sit with has
   probably been burned by a program that guessed, changed something
   silently, or lost work. That story is the most precise statement of our
   own house rules I will ever be handed, in a translator's words rather
   than mine.

Three outcomes are all legitimate results and must be written down as
such: *build this*, *cut that*, and **"this person should carry on working
exactly as they do"**. The third is a finding, not a failure. If what they
actually need is a typist, a better lamp, a photocopy of a dictionary or
an introduction to someone, I should say so in the notes and, where I can,
simply give it to them with no tool attached.

**Standing rule on quotation.** Nothing anyone says is quoted with their
name attached unless I go back and ask them for that specific sentence.
Notes are anonymised the same day. Say this out loud at the start; say it
again in the write-up.

## Preparing for this particular person

Fifteen minutes before each session, run this checklist. Nine sessions
with the same script would be nine wasted hours.

- [ ] **Read their questionnaire reply, if they sent one.** Note the three
      things they said hurt most and the wishes they listed — I am here to
      go behind those answers, not collect them a second time. Where their
      reply is thin or evasive, that section is where to spend the hour.
- [ ] **If they did not reply**, note that and do not mention it. Some of
      the best informants are the ones who did not want to write.
- [ ] **Know which texts they work on**, roughly what stage those are at,
      and who else touches them. Being able to say the name of the text
      they are living inside is worth more than any preamble.
- [ ] **Know their register in the team** — new, long-serving, teaching,
      editing, publishing. It sets the length, the fears, and which
      sections below matter.
- [ ] **Guess, in one line, what kind of person I am about to meet**, and
      hold the guess loosely:
      - *Newer* — likely to round their times down, name the respectable
        resources rather than the ones they open, and call a guess a
        working choice. Spend the hour on where they get stuck and what
        happens to a question before it is asked. Sixty minutes maximum;
        honesty decays after fifty.
      - *Long-serving* — has a working system and nothing to gain. Spend
        the hour watching, and on their glossary, their error signature,
        and what they would turn off. They may be worth ninety minutes;
        offer, do not assume.
      - *Paper-first or software-shy* — will be polite instead of honest.
        No screen share, probably no laptop at all; spend the hour on the
        desk, the notebook, the scar, and the map of where a tool must not
        come.
- [ ] **Have one honest weakness of the tool ready to name**, and one real
      thing already cut because somebody objected. These are what buy the
      candour later.
- [ ] **Book the room, not the demo.** Notebook and two pens. Laptop in
      the bag.

## Session shape

**Length: sixty minutes, hard stop, announced at the start and honoured to
the minute.** Say at minute zero: *"we stop at [time] whatever happens —
you will not have to find a way to end this."* Then stop at that time even
mid-sentence, and ask whether I may write with one more question. Ending
on time — or early — buys more trust than anything I could demonstrate. If
it is going well, book a second sitting rather than stealing the extra
half hour. Ninety minutes only for someone long-serving who offers it,
and even then stop at eighty-eight.

**Where: their desk, their machine, at the hour they would have been
translating anyway.** Not my office, not a neutral slot carved out of the
evening. If they work at six in the morning before their job, meet at six.
The tiredness and the interruptions are part of the finding. In person if
travel allows — I learn more from ten seconds of the desk than ten minutes
of description. If not in person, video for someone comfortable with it;
**voice only for a paper worker**, with them sitting at their own desk so
they can look at their pages while they talk.

**Screen share: theirs, one direction, and only if they use a screen.** My
screen stays dark for the first forty minutes. Ask for it in these words:
*"I want to see the mess — please don't tidy anything before we meet, the
tidying is exactly what hides the thing I'm trying to fix."* Say twice: do
not close tabs, rename files, or prepare a clean version. Give an explicit
out — they may stop sharing at any moment, no reason needed, and I will
not ask why. **Never ask a paper worker to share a screen**; for them the
request itself is an accusation. Invite them instead to hold up a page or
push a notebook across the table, and ask permission per page before any
photograph.

**Recording: no, by default.** Hand notes in a paper notebook, and say so:
*"I'm not recording. I'll write things down badly and send you what I
wrote so you can cross out what I got wrong."* Writing by hand also mirrors
the medium of anyone who works on paper. If audio would genuinely help and
the person is comfortable with it, ask **on the day, out loud**, even if
they agreed by email; agree the phrase *"off the record"*, demonstrate the
stop button once so it is real, name a deletion date, and honour the first
use of the phrase visibly. Treat the first hesitation as a no and never
ask again.

**Who is in the room: two people.** No second engineer, no note-taker, no
colleague who would also find it interesting, no other translator, not the
editor, not Geshe Michael. Say this out loud, and say the negative too:
this is not an assessment, nothing goes back to the class or to Geshe
Michael with their name on it, and it has nothing to do with what they get
assigned next.

**My laptop stays in the bag** for the first forty minutes, then comes out
closed on the table so they can see that it is closed. It opens only after
asking, and only on a real yes. **If it never opens, the session still
succeeded.**

**The ratio.** I talk less than a quarter of the hour. If I cross a third,
the interview has become a demonstration and the data is already spoiled.
Put a watch where I can see it.

**Shape of the sixty minutes**

| | |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Opening and permission-setting |
| 5–15 | Their day before the Tibetan starts |
| 15–35 | Watching them work, or walking through their last page |
| 35–45 | The questions their work raised — sticking points, terms, glossary, checking, hand-off |
| 45–48 | Three wishes, asked **before** anything is shown |
| 48–57 | I show at most three things, on their own passage — or nothing at all |
| 57–60 | Close, consent, and one next step |

**If time collapses, this is the spine** (about eighteen questions fit in
an hour; the ones marked ★ below are these):

- take me from "I'm going to work on this" to the first syllable
- now just work, and let me watch
- when you don't know a word, what does your hand go to first
- how do you find out how Geshe Michael rendered a term
- where's your first wall — the letters, the grammar, the meaning, or the English
- show me the last passage that beat you
- walk me through the last term you weren't sure of — what did you write
- show me your glossary, as it is
- what check do you skip because it's tedious
- what kind of mistake do you make
- when he answers a question, what happens to the answer
- where should a computer never appear
- what's the bit of your system I'd be a fool to disturb
- three wishes, and then only one

**Two sessions beat one.** Session A is this one: watch and ask, nothing
installed. Session B, thirty minutes a week or two later: they translate
with a build in hand and I say nothing while it gets in the way.

## What to ask them to have open (or on the desk)

Send this three days ahead as four sentences, and phrase every item so the
low-effort version is acceptable. Say the word *unprepared* out loud as the
goal.

- **A real passage they are in the middle of** and still unsure about —
  roughly half an hour of work left in it. Explicitly not a finished
  showpiece. *"If you have something that's currently beating you, bring
  that."*
- **Their glossary, term list or notebook**, in whatever state. *"Especially
  if it's a mess. If it doesn't exist, that's also an answer and I want to
  know it."*
- **The pages they worked on last week**, marks, crossings-out and coffee
  rings included.
- **The one book they reach for most**, and anything that lives on paper
  beside the keyboard.
- **The last three things that made them want to close the laptop.**
- **Nothing else. No preparation. No tidying.**

## The opening five minutes

These five minutes decide the other fifty-five. The goal is not rapport in
general; it is to make honesty cheaper than courtesy before a single
question is asked.

**1. Disarm the room, physically (30 seconds).** Sit down, notebook on the
table, laptop bag on the floor. *"I'm not going to show you anything for
the first while, and I'm not going to ask you to use anything. My laptop is
staying in the bag. I mostly want to listen."* For someone braced for a
demonstration, watching the laptop go on the floor does more than any
sentence.

**2. Say what the hour is really for, and prove it with a cut (60
seconds).** *"I've built something and I'm worried I've built the wrong
thing. The most useful thing you can give me is what to throw away."* Then
name two specific things already cut or changed because somebody objected.
Anyone can ask for honest feedback; only someone who has actually deleted
work can show that the feedback lands somewhere.

**3. Go first, with something real (60 seconds).** Level the status gap by
spending my own. Not false modesty — a specific, checkable admission: the
feature that was stupid, the three days I spent certain a word was missing
from the dictionary when I had simply spelt it wrong. *"Whatever you tell
me you can't do, I promise it's less embarrassing than that."*

**4. Give them permission to be rude, and a word to be rude with (45
seconds).** *"You're a courteous person and I'm going to ask you to be a bit
uncourteous for an hour. If something sounds like nonsense or a waste of
your time, just say 'that one's not for me.' You don't have to explain, and
I won't argue or try to talk you round. I'll write it down and we'll move
on."* Get them to say the phrase back, half-joking, so it exists. This one
device is the difference between an hour of *"mm, that's interesting"* and
an hour of usable truth.

**5. Pre-forgive the specific admissions (45 seconds).** Name in advance
the things people are most afraid to say, so that saying them is joining a
category rather than confessing. *"Just so it's said out loud: looking
things up constantly is normal, being slow is normal, using ChatGPT is
normal and I'm not policing it, and putting something down when you're not
sure and hoping nobody notices is normal and roughly half of what I'm
trying to fix."*

**6. Set the terms, including the exit (45 seconds).** One hour, ending at
a named time. Not recorded; hand notes only, and they get them back to
correct. **Nothing quoted with their name unless I come back and ask about
that sentence.** Nothing to install, nothing to sign, no obligation at the
end. Then the sentence that matters most, which they will test later:
*"If by the end you tell me the best thing this tool can do for you is stay
out of your way — that's a real answer, I'll write it down as a result, and
it will change what I build. I'm not here to convert anybody."*

**7. Hand them the floor with a question that has nothing to do with
computers (45 seconds).** *"Tell me what you're translating at the moment.
What is the text, and how did you come to be the one working on it?"* Then
stop talking and let the silence run. The first long answer establishes who
the expert in the room is, and it is not me.

**One disclosure I make myself, unprompted, rather than letting it be
discovered:** the tool has exactly one feature that sends text off the
machine to an AI service; it does nothing unless a button is pressed; its
output is banner-labelled as machine-written; and everything else — the
dictionary, the corpus, search, conversions — runs offline on their own
disk with no account and no reporting. For a person burned by software,
being told the uncomfortable thing before they find it is worth more than
the feature itself.

**Leave out of the opening:** the tool's name and what it does (that
arrives at minute forty, if at all), the corpus size, how long I have been
building, and any sentence beginning *"I know you prefer paper, but…"* —
which tells them the argument has already started.

**Tonal rules for the whole hour.** Ask about *the last time*, never *the
usual time*. Ask what they *did*, never what they *do*. Offer the large
answer first so the true one isn't shrunk (*"was that more like an hour, or
more like three?"*). Presuppose difficulty rather than asking whether it
exists (*"where does the word-boundary thing bite you?"*, not *"do you find
word boundaries hard?"*). Never use the words *problem*, *struggle* or
*difficulty* about them — use *time*, and *what happened*, and *show me*.
And after an admission, do not reassure: reassurance closes the topic. Say
*"that's useful — tell me more about that one"*, and wait.

## The questions

★ marks the spine. Everything else is optional. Follow whatever they
actually open up about; the sections run in the order of their day so I can
navigate quickly when the conversation jumps.

### A. Who they are, and what burned them

**A1. How long have you been translating, and who taught you?**
Puts them in the expert's chair for the first ten minutes, which is the
only position from which a wary person tells the truth. It also tells me
whose conventions bind this person — which teacher, which glossary, which
house they were raised in — and that governs everything they say about
terms later.
- What was the first text you worked all the way through?
- What did your teacher insist on that you still do today?
- Has the way you work changed much since then?

**A2. Tell me about a piece of software that wasted your time.**
Asked as an invitation to complain about someone else's program, it turns
the wariness in the room into a shared grievance and names, in their words,
the exact failure I must not repeat. Never ask *do you like computers*; ask
for the story of a specific injury.
- What did it do, exactly, and when did you notice?
- What did you have to do to put it right?
- Was there a moment you decided you were finished with it?

**A3. Has a program ever changed something in your work without you
noticing until later?**
This is the guessing scar, and the direct test of our rules 1 and 3.
Whatever they describe — a silent autocorrect, a dropped diacritic, a
helpful substitution — is the concrete standard the tool will be judged
against, and it is far more precise than any promise about provenance.
- How did you find out in the end?
- What would it have had to do instead for you to have forgiven it?
- Do you check for that now? How?

**A4. Have you ever been shown a machine translation of Tibetan? What did
you think of it?**
The fear is in the room whether or not it is spoken, and it is better
spoken. My job here is to listen and write; this is the question where the
urge to defend will be strongest and must be most firmly resisted.
- Was it wrong in a way you could see, or in a way you'd have to check?
- Does it worry you that this work is being done by machines at all?

### B. Before you start

**B1. ★ Take me from "I'm going to work on this text" to the first
syllable. What actually happens in between — all of it, including "I made
tea and stalled for twenty minutes"?**
The setup ritual is invisible labour nobody has ever described aloud, and
it decides whether an app that adds ten minutes of setup is dead on
arrival. Naming the stalling inside the question makes it sayable.
- How long from sitting down to the first English word on the page?
- What had to be open before you felt ready?
- What would break if you skipped a step? Which step is the same every time?

**B2. How does the Tibetan reach you, and what state is it in when it
arrives?**
Intake decides what import must handle and how much unpaid preparation sits
in front of the real work. A photocopy from a class binder, a printed
pecha, a BDRC scan, a file a student sent — each implies a different point
of entry, or none.
- What did you have to do to it before you could start?
- Have you ever retyped Tibetan by hand just to be able to work with it?
- Has it ever arrived in a state you couldn't work from?

**B3. What time of day do you translate, and what has to be true before you
can start?**
Establishes the conditions of the work, which are usually protective and
rarely volunteered — quiet, a particular light, an empty house, an hour
before anyone is awake. Anything that violates those conditions is dead on
arrival however useful it is.
- How long before you're properly in it? What breaks it?
- How often are you interrupted mid-passage?
- Coming back after two days, how do you find your place and remember what
  you'd already decided?

**B4. Is there anything you do before you begin that would look like
superstition to an outsider?**
On this team the sitting may open with practice, offering or prayer, and
that is not decoration — it is the frame that makes the work what it is.
Asked with a half-smile, this maps the most important boundary in the
session: the part of the day where a machine must never appear.
- Does that carry on while you work, or only at the start?
- Is the translating itself practice for you, or is it work?
- Would having a screen open change any of that?

**B5. Walk me round the desk — and show me the screen setup. What's on it
at once?**
The desk is the honest inventory: what they name when asked which
dictionaries they use and what is physically within arm's reach are
different lists, and the second one is true. Screen real estate is also the
binding constraint on a single-window application, and a beginner on one
thirteen-inch laptop juggling six windows is a completely different design
problem from someone with two monitors.
- Go clockwise — which of these do you touch every single time?
- Which window is the master, the one you always come back to? Which two do
  you flip between most?
- What's on paper, and why is that thing on paper?

**B6. Do you choose what you translate, or is it handed to you?**
Surfaces whether they have ever been given something above their level and
said nothing — a common and entirely unreported source of despair.
- Has anything been assigned that you privately thought was over your head?
  What did you do?
- Do you skip hard passages and come back, or grind through in order?
- Has anyone asked how it was going, and did you tell them the truth?

**B7. Is there anything you have to do first that you resent?**
A resented ritual is a workaround normalised long enough to stop being
reported as a problem.
- How long has it been that way? Have you tried to fix it?
- What stopped you?

### C. The sitting itself — watch, do not interview

**C1. ★ Now just work. Translate this the way you would if I weren't here,
and say whatever comes into your head. If you go quiet because you're
thinking, that's fine — I'll ask you about it afterwards.**
Everything else in this guide is self-report; this is the only evidence.
Twenty minutes of watching is worth more than the rest of the session.
**For a paper worker, the equivalent is the artefact walk:** *"Can I see the
last page you worked on? Just talk me through what's on it."* Ask about the
marks, not the meaning of the text — every mark is a decision a tool would
either serve or destroy.
- (afterwards) At about eight minutes you stopped for a while. What was
  happening there?
- (afterwards) You went straight to that window without saying why — what
  were you checking?
- (afterwards) You typed something and deleted it. What was wrong with it?
- (paper) What does this mark mean, and this one? Which did you make on the
  first pass? Is there anything here you'd be annoyed to lose?

**C2. When a new sentence appears, do you read the whole thing first, or
start at the first word and move along? How do you find the verb?**
Experienced translators scan for the verb automatically and have forgotten
learning it. Whether that habit has formed decides whether the tool must
scaffold sentence-shape or stay out of the way — and whether the drafting
surface should be whole-passage or clause-at-a-time.
- When you look at a long sentence, where do your eyes actually go first?
- Do you parse before you draft, or draft and then repair?
- Does that change when the passage is hard?

**C3. Do you read the Tibetan aloud?**
A one-word answer with large consequences. If yes, sound is part of
comprehension, and pronunciation stops being a nicety for students and
becomes part of the translator's own instrument. Almost nobody volunteers
it.
- Out loud, or under your breath?
- Does the sound of it ever settle a reading for you?
- Do you do the same with your English?

**C4. How long is one sitting, and how much gets done in it — a folio, a
page, a line?**
The most important number in the session and the one most likely to be
shrunk out of embarrassment; listing the units from large down to *a line*
makes the small answer respectable. It also quietly tests whether speed is
even the currency this person deals in.
- Was the last one typical, or a good day?
- Is a good day more lines, or fewer lines done better?
- What's the longest a single line has ever taken you?
- Would getting through twice as much actually be good?

**C5. Where does the English go while you're working — the same page, a
facing page, a separate file?**
The geometry of Tibetan and English is the thing every drafting bench in
the world tries to imitate, usually badly. Whatever they have arrived at
over years is the specification, and the reason they chose it matters more
than the layout.
- Why that way rather than the other?
- How much space do you leave yourself, and what fills it?
- Do you ever end up with two or three Englishes stacked for one line?

**C6. Of an hour at this, how much is actually translating, and how much is
typing, formatting, hunting for files, wrestling with Word?**
Separates the intellectual work from the mechanical tax. The mechanical tax
is the only part a tool can honestly delete.
- What's the fiddliest mechanical thing you do?
- Do you type Tibetan yourself? With what keyboard?
- What do you copy and paste most often, and between what?

**C7. When does any of this get typed, and who types it?**
The single most important integration question for anyone working on
paper. The paper-to-screen boundary is where a tool could help without
intruding — and if a spouse, a student or an assistant does the typing, the
real user of any such tool is somebody I have not met.
- What happens between your page and the typed file — does anything get
  lost or changed?
- Do you check the typed version against your page? How?
- If typing were free and instant, would that change how you work on paper?

**C8. You just chose between two English words. How did you choose?**
The centre of tacit expertise. The answer is usually about register, the
Geshe's voice, or who is reading — none of which a machine may ever decide
for them.
- Would you choose differently if this were a prayer rather than prose?
- Has a choice like that ever been overturned by someone else?
- Could you write down the rule you just used?

**C9. How much of that passage could you have done with nothing open at
all?**
Calibrates how much of the tool's value is lookup and how much is
everything else. For a long-serving translator the answer is often *most of
it*, which is the most important design fact I can leave with.
- Which bits genuinely needed looking up?
- Was that true ten years ago?
- What will you never be able to do from memory?

**C10. Tell me about the last time you stopped early. What made you stop?**
Quitting points are the design targets and nobody volunteers them; asking
for one instance makes it a story rather than a confession.
- Tiredness, a hard passage, or something not working?
- Did you go back to it? How long after?
- How often does that happen in a month?

### D. What you reach for

**D1. ★ When you meet a word you don't know, what does your hand go to
first? Then second, then third — the actual order, not the respectable
one.**
Asked as a physical question — hand, not mind — it gets the true order. The
first reach is the function a tool must beat or leave alone; everything
after it is where a tool has room.
- Which do you trust most, and which do you reach for most? Are they the
  same one?
- How far down that list do you usually get, and how long does the whole
  business take on a bad word?
- Which did you find out about from a friend rather than being taught?

**D2. Show me the actual things that are open right now — the tabs, the
books, the files. Not what you usually use.**
Reported frequency is unreliable; the artefacts are not. Expect a mismatch
with what they said a minute ago, and note it without pointing it out.
Physical distance is an honest frequency count: a book they get up for three
times a session is a pain they have stopped noticing.
- Which of these could you not work without?
- Which do you open from habit and rarely get anything from?
- Which is furthest away, and when do you actually get up for it?

**D3. Which reference do you trust most, and for which kind of question? Is
there one you don't trust — what's wrong with it?**
Trust ordering, not frequency, is what should decide how stacked results
are ranked and labelled. And their reasons for distrusting a dictionary —
too old, compiled from other dictionaries, no citations, someone else's
tradition — are exactly the criteria they will silently apply to my tool.
- Where does each one let you down?
- How did you find out it wasn't reliable?
- Does it matter to you who made a dictionary, and when?

**D4. ★ When you need to know how Geshe Michael himself rendered a term,
what do you actually do? Walk me through the last time, including whether
you found it.**
This is the tool's entire reason for existing, and I need to know precisely
what manual labour it would be replacing — or whether they attempt it at
all any more. *"Including whether you found it"* makes failure a normal
outcome to report rather than an admission.
- How long did that take, start to finish?
- How often do you give up and choose your own English instead?
- If you find one instance, is that enough, or do you want to see several?
- When you can't find his, does anyone downstream know you didn't?

**D5. Is there a paper book on your desk right now? Why hasn't a screen
replaced it?**
Paper survives for specific reasons worth designing for — browsing, layout,
one's own marginalia — and if a printed glossary of his is at their elbow,
the bar for replacing it is very high and probably should not be attempted.
- Do you write in it? Have you tabbed or indexed it yourself?
- What would a screen have to do before you'd shut it?

**D6. How do you search? Say out loud the last thing you typed into a
search box.**
Search-string quality is a pure hidden tax: a misspelling returns nothing,
and *nothing* gets read as *the word isn't in there*. I need the exact
strings, and the failed query is more informative than the successful one
because it shows what they expected the tool to understand.
- When a search comes back empty, what do you conclude?
- Have you ever found out later that the word was there and you'd spelt it
  differently?
- Do you search in Wylie, ACIP or Tibetan script? Which do you avoid?

**D7. Do you use any AI at any point — ChatGPT, Claude, Google Translate?
I'm not asking to catch anyone. I need to know what my tool is actually
competing with.**
Enormously under-reported by anyone speaking to someone in Geshe Michael's
circle, and it decides whether the tool competes with a chatbot. The
disclaimer must be said before the question lands. The gentler general form
— *"what do you use that you'd be slightly embarrassed to admit using?"* —
also catches the doubtful PDF, the search engine, and a student's notes.
- What do you use it for — rough sense, the grammar, polishing the English?
- What has it got wrong that you caught? What might it have got wrong that
  you didn't?
- Would you tell the class you'd used it? Why not?
- Would you mind if the tool did that same thing openly?

**D8. When the books fail, who do you ask?**
The human layer is invisible to software projects and is usually the
most-used tool in the room. Sometimes the honest answer is not a program
but a faster way to reach a person.
- How long do you sit with something before you ask? What does asking cost
  you?
- Do you save questions up? How many are waiting right now?
- Is there anything you'd rather look up than ask a person, because asking
  costs something?

### E. Where you get stuck

**E1. ★ Everyone has a different first wall. Some people can't tell where a
word starts and ends. Some read every word and still don't get the
sentence. Some get the sentence and can't make it into English. Where's
yours?**
Offering three named, respectable failure modes turns admitting one into
joining a category. These are four completely different tools, and building
the wrong one is the most expensive mistake available here.
- Which of those has moved since you started, and which hasn't moved at all?
- Which costs the most time? Which do you actually enjoy?
- Show me a place in this passage where it happened.

**E2. ★ Show me the last passage that beat you. Not one you solved — one
you're still not sure about.**
An unsolved passage is worth ten solved ones, and asking for it directly
makes not-knowing the purpose of the exercise rather than a confession made
during it. This passage becomes a permanent test fixture.
- What's sitting in your draft where that passage should be?
- Does anyone besides you know that line is uncertain?
- What would have unstuck it — a word, a person, an example, more time?
- Was that a normal one, or unusually bad?

**E3. Is the Tibetan ever hard just to read — the letters themselves, the
stacked ones, small print, a bad scan, cursive?**
The tax nobody admits, because it feels like a failure of skill when it is
a typography and eyesight problem.
- Do you zoom in, or change the font? Has anyone shown you how?
- Have you ever misread a letter and only found out much later?

**E4. The little particles. Do you always know which one you're looking at
and what job it's doing?**
The eight-case *la* is the standard wall. People nod along in class and
cannot do it live, and will not say so unless asked about the particle
specifically rather than about grammar in general.
- Which particle gives you the most trouble?
- When you're not sure, do you translate around it and hope?
- If the tool named its job on the page, would that help — or would you
  stop learning it?

**E5. How often do you finish a sentence and think, I've got all the words
but I don't know what he's actually saying?**
The comprehension gap is distinct from the vocabulary gap and badly
under-reported, because it feels like a failure of the practice rather than
of the tools. *How often* presupposes that it happens.
- What do you do then?
- Is it usually a point of doctrine you haven't been taught, or the grammar?
- Does the English you write in that case come out sounding more confident
  than you feel?

**E6. Names, places, text titles, someone being quoted — how do you handle
those?**
Proper nouns look exactly like ordinary words, and nobody thinks to warn
anyone because it stopped being a hazard decades ago.
- Have you ever translated a name by accident and caught it later?
- How do you find out who a person is, once you know it's a person?
- How do you tell a quotation from the author's own words?

**E7. What do you do when the typed text and the woodblock disagree, or a
word isn't in any dictionary at all?**
Reveals whether they have a method or an improvisation for the hardest case
in Tibetan work. It is also the point where a tool is most tempted to
guess, so their tolerance for *the machine offers a plausible reading* is
measurable right here.
- Can you get to another edition when you need one? How?
- Do you record that there was a disagreement, or settle it and move on?
- Would you want to know that other people had hit the same line?

**E8. What do you do with a passage you can't finish? Where does it
physically go?**
Finds the parked-question backlog — a margin question mark, a folded
corner, a list at the back of the notebook. It is a queue with no lid on
it, and its cost is invisible until counted out loud.
- How many are open right now, roughly? How do you find them all again?
- How do you decide to go back to one?
- Has one ever got out into a finished text?

**E9. Is there something you feel you should know by now and don't —
something you'd rather not admit in class?**
This is the question the whole session exists to earn the right to ask,
which is why it comes here and not earlier. Ask it slowly, then say nothing
for as long as it takes.
- How do you get around it day to day?
- How many times have you looked it up without it sticking?
- Would you want a tool that quietly knew you keep forgetting this, or
  would that feel like being watched?

**E10. ★ What do you look up over and over — words you've looked up ten
times and still don't hold?**
An easier door into the same room as E9, and it specifies a feature almost
exactly: a personal recurring-lookup list.
- Do you keep a list of those? Would you show me?
- Is it embarrassing to look one up for the tenth time?
- What would you want to happen on the eleventh?

### F. When a term is uncertain

**F1. ★ Walk me through the last term you weren't sure about — from the
moment of doubt to the decision. What did you actually write?**
The decision under uncertainty is the tool's central case, and *"had to put
something down anyway"* pre-forgives the guess before they have to describe
it.
- What evidence did you accept in the end? What would have changed your
  mind?
- Did you mark it in any way, or did it go in looking like ordinary English?
- Would anyone reading your draft be able to tell you weren't sure?
- Roughly how many times does that happen on a page?

**F2. Do you have any way of flagging your own doubt — brackets, a question
mark, a colour, a note in the margin?**
Determines whether uncertainty survives to the page or is flattened into
confident English. If it does not survive, the pipeline is losing its most
valuable signal.
- Do you take those marks out before anyone sees it? What makes you take
  them out?
- Has anyone ever asked to see the doubts rather than the answer?
- What would make you comfortable leaving them in?

**F3. When a dictionary gives you five English options, or two dictionaries
disagree, how do you pick?**
Shows what kind of ranking would help and what would offend — in particular
whether counts of attested usage read as evidence or as pressure.
- Does it matter how often he used a given rendering? Does it matter which
  text it came from?
- Does the audience for this passage change it?
- Has it ever turned out later that you picked wrong?

**F4. When the same term needs different English in a prayer and in prose,
how do you decide?**
The register question is the deepest correctness issue in the data and the
one place the tool must refuse to decide. Their reasoning is the human rule
the tool should surface rather than resolve.
- Give me one you keep having to think about. Have you ever got it the
  wrong way round?
- Do you think of it as him changing his mind, or as two different
  situations?
- Would seeing both side by side help you, or slow you down?

**F5. Have you ever used a word because it was the one you remembered, and
later found he'd used something else?**
Establishes the error rate of memory without insulting the memory. If they
say yes, they have just described the problem the corpus solves in their
own words. If they say no, that is a finding too, and I must accept it.
- How did you find out? Did it matter, in the end?
- Would you have wanted to know at the time, or would that have interrupted
  you?

**F6. When do you keep the Tibetan or Sanskrit word rather than translating
it?**
House convention affecting italics, diacritics, first-use glossing and the
glossary itself — and it varies by person more than anyone admits. Newer
translators quietly invent a policy because nobody wrote one down.
- Who decided that — you, the editor, or the Geshe?
- Is it written down anywhere, or did you pick it up by watching?
- What would you want to be able to check without having to ask a person?

**F7. Have you ever changed your mind about a term you'd already used a
hundred times? What happened then?**
The retro-fix problem is enormous, invisible, and never volunteered — a
whole-volume change needing judgement at every hit.
- How did you find all the places? How sure were you you'd got them all?
- Did anything break as a result?

**F8. Suppose every place he ever used a term were laid out in front of
you, with the sentence around each one and nothing added. What would you do
with that?**
Phrased as *what would you do* rather than *would you like it*, so the
answer is a behaviour and not a compliment. If the honest answer is *"I'd
have to read fifty sentences and I haven't got time"*, that is the finding.
- How many would be useful before it became too many?
- Would you want them in some order? What order?
- In front of you while working, or only when you're stuck?

**F9. ★ If a machine had no answer for a term, what should it do — say
nothing, say plainly that it doesn't know, or offer you its best guess?**
The direct test of rule 3, put to the person best qualified to answer it.
Their words become quotable ammunition in every future argument about
filling a blank. Ask for it verbatim.
- What should a blank look like so you'd notice it and not read past it?
- Is there any circumstance where you'd want a guess, clearly labelled?
- What if it could tell you how sure it was — does that help, or is that a
  dressed-up guess?

**F10. If it offered you an English word that nobody in our tradition had
ever used for that term, would you want to see it?**
Draws the sharpest line in the house rules — matched from evidence versus
composed by machine — and lets the translator draw it rather than me. The
reaction is often visceral, and it is exactly what I need when someone
later proposes a helpful-suggestion feature.
- Does it change anything if it's clearly marked as the machine's own words?
- Where does the harm come from — that it's wrong, or that it might stick?
- Is there anywhere it would be all right — a scratch pad, a private note?

### G. How you record decisions

**G1. ★ Show me your glossary — not a description of it, the actual thing,
as it is. Especially if it's a mess.**
For a long-serving translator this is a life's work and the closest thing
that exists to a specification. For a newer one there may be nothing, or
something they are ashamed of, and *especially if it's a mess* is the
permission clause that makes the file appear. **Look; do not touch.**
- How old is it? What's in one row? What's the most recent thing you added?
- Is it one list, several, or per text?
- Do you actually read it while translating, or only write to it?

**G2. How did it start, and how has its shape changed?**
Its history is a record of pain: every column was added after something
went wrong.
- What did you add after a mistake? What have you stopped keeping?
- Is there a column you wish you'd started ten years ago?

**G3. What's in it besides the term and the English?**
The notes, dates, sources, doubt markers and *who said so* fields are the
valuable part, and precisely what a naive import would destroy.
- Do you record where a decision came from? How sure you were? Who approved
  it?
- Do you write down *why* you chose something, or only *what*?
- Would you record the reason if it were one keystroke?

**G4. How do you add a row while you're in the middle of translating?**
The friction of capture is why glossaries die; the gap between decisions
made and decisions recorded is where the team's knowledge leaks out.
- Do you ever skip it because it's too much bother mid-flow?
- How many that you meant to add later actually got added?
- How long does adding one take?

**G5. How do you find out what you decided three months ago?**
Retrieval is where these systems genuinely fail, and the failure has
usually been accepted as a fact of life rather than named as a problem.
- When did you last go looking for one? Did you find it?
- What do you do when you can't — decide again from scratch?
- Have you ended up rendering one term two ways inside a single text? How
  did you notice, or did someone else?

**G6. Is there a second place decisions live — your head, a margin, an
email thread?**
The uncaptured remainder is the real state of the team's knowledge, and it
is usually larger than the file.
- Roughly how much of it is only in your head?
- What happens when you forget one?
- Has anyone else ever needed something that was only in your head?

**G7. If it vanished tonight, what exactly would you lose?**
Gets its true value stated in their own words and quietly surfaces whether
it is backed up — which, often, it is not. The most useful thing I could
offer this person may be a safe copy of what they have already made.
- Is there a copy anywhere? Could you rebuild it? How long would that take?
- Would you want it copied, if it cost you nothing and changed nothing
  about how you work?

**G8. Does anyone else see it? Would you want them to?**
Decides whether shared-glossary features are a gift or an intrusion. Ask;
do not assume the generous answer.
- Have you shared it before, and how did that go?
- What in it would you never want shared?
- Would you want to see someone else's?

**G9. Does the Tibetan stay next to your English anywhere, or does it drop
away once the line is done?**
Whether that link survives determines whether checking, review, or any
later defence of a line is possible at all.
- If someone questioned a line, could you get back to the exact Tibetan?
- How, and how long would it take? Has that ever happened?

### H. How you check yourself

**H1. Before you call a passage finished, what do you actually do? Take me
through the last one.**
The self-review checklist is real, unwritten, and the closest thing to a
specification for a pre-hand-off report. Asking about a specific passage
removes the yes/no dodge from anyone who has no checking pass at all.
- Do you read the English on its own, out loud, without the Tibetan? Or go
  back word by word against the Tibetan?
- Is it the same steps every time? Same day, or the next?
- Which of those could someone else do for you, and which could nobody?

**H2. ★ Is there a check you know you ought to do and don't, because it's
too tedious?**
The best question in the section. A check they already believe in but skip
is automation they have pre-approved — no persuasion needed, no risk of
building something unasked-for, and the least threatening kind of help to
offer.
- What makes it tedious — the counting, the flipping back, the looking up?
- How often does skipping it cost you something?
- If it were done for you, would you want the result on paper or on screen?

**H3. ★ What kind of mistake do you make? Not the worst one — the one that
repeats.**
Knowing one's own error signature is the most useful thing anyone can hand
me, and the honest basis for a personal checklist rather than a generic
one. *Not the worst one* lowers the stakes enough to get a true answer.
- How did you find that out about yourself — did someone tell you, or did
  you notice?
- Do you check for it deliberately now? Has it changed over the years?
- Would you want a machine pointing those out, or would that grate?

**H4. How do you know you haven't dropped a line, or a negative?**
Omission and reversed polarity are the classic silent errors; whatever they
do about them is what any back-check has to beat.
- Has that happened to you? How did you find out?
- Has one particular kind ever got all the way through to print?

**H5. Do you come back and read it cold later? How much later?**
Establishes whether the process has a resting period, which decides whether
a report should arrive immediately or be waiting when they sit down to
revise. Interrupting mid-flow is intrusion; a list at revision time is
service.
- What do you see on the cold read that you didn't see before?
- Do you read your English on paper or on screen for that?

**H6. How do you know when you're finished, as opposed to just tired?**
The honest definition of done is often *I ran out of evening*. Naming
tiredness in the question makes the real answer available.
- Has anything gone off that you knew wasn't finished?
- Is there a standard you're measuring against, or is it a feeling?
- What would make you more confident saying done?

### I. What happens to the finished passage

**I1. Where does it go when you're done, in what form, and to whom?**
The hand-off boundary is where formats are converted by hand and where work
is silently lost — and it is the point at which their work leaves their
control, which is where most fears about tools live.
- What do they need from you that you have to prepare manually?
- Do you tidy before sending — take out notes, brackets, doubts? What
  exactly comes out?
- How long before you hear anything back?

**I2. What comes back, and what do you do with it?**
The return loop matters more than the hand-off: whether corrections reach
their glossary decides whether the same argument happens twice. If nothing
comes back at all, they are translating into a void and a modest *here's
what changed* would be worth more than any dictionary feature.
- Do you understand every change made to your draft? When you don't, do you
  ask?
- Do edits ever change a term you'd already decided on? Does your glossary
  get updated?
- Do you get asked before something is changed, or told after?

**I3. How do you know which version is the current one?**
Versioning pain is universal and never volunteered; the answer is usually a
filename convention with a story behind it.
- How many copies of this file exist right now?
- Have you ever worked on the wrong one?
- How would someone else know which is current?

**I4. Has anything you translated been published? How did it look compared
to what you sent?**
A generous question that usually produces pride, and pride produces
candour. It also surfaces the last mile — footnotes, diacritics, a mangled
Tibetan font — where injuries are frequent, specific, and fixable by
unglamorous software.
- Was there anything in it you didn't recognise as yours?
- Did anyone explain the changes, or did you have to infer them?
- What did you learn from it that nobody actually said out loud?

**I5. Is there a version of your work you'd never show anybody?**
Names the private draft — the one with the guesses still in it — which is
where the honest data is, and surfaces a real requirement: a place that is
explicitly private.
- What's in it that you take out before anyone sees?
- Would a place guaranteed private help, or would it just be another file?

### J. When you disagree with a published rendering

**J1. ★ Has there ever been a published English rendering that didn't match
what you were seeing in the Tibetan, or didn't fit the passage in front of
you?**
The most delicate question in the guide and the most important for our
honesty rules. It has to be asked without any hint that the right answer is
*no*. Record it exactly; do not defend the rule and do not negotiate it in
the room.
- What did you do — follow it, quietly do it your own way, footnote it, or
  raise it?
- Did you assume you were the one who was wrong? How long before you
  decided that?
- How did it sit with you afterwards? Has it happened more than once?

**J2. If you thought a rendering ought to be changed, how would you go
about it? Who decides?**
Maps the real authority path, which any proposal or approval mechanism must
follow rather than invent. If there is no path, that is a finding about the
team, not about the software, and should be reported as such.
- Is there a way to raise it, or does it depend on catching the right
  person?
- Has anything you raised ever actually changed? How long did it take?
- With your name on it, or anonymous? To a person, or into a queue? Would
  you want to find out what happened to it?

**J3. If a tool put his rendering in front of you every time you worked on
a line, what would that feel like?**
Names the surveillance fear directly instead of letting it colour every
other answer. Some find it a comfort, some a supervisor at the shoulder,
and the answer decides whether that display is always on, on request, or
off by default.
- Would it matter whether anyone else could see that you'd disagreed?
- Is it different when translating for a class rather than for print?
- While you're drafting, or only when you're checking?

**J4. If you could see every place he used a term and how often each
English appears, would that make you more likely to speak up, or less?**
This genuinely goes either way: evidence can empower or intimidate. The
answer decides how counts and concordances are presented.
- Would you want to see the rare renderings too, or only the common one?
- Would a rendering used only once make you trust it less?
- Does it matter whether it's from twenty years ago or last year? Would you
  want the date and source shown?

### K. The class, the team, and Geshe Michael

**K1. How does the class fit into your translating — before, during,
after?**
The tool sits inside a teaching relationship; ignoring it solves a third of
the problem. Class work and print work also have completely different
tolerances, and a tool that serves one may burden the other.
- Are you ahead of the class or behind it? Where does the pressure come
  from?
- Do you use class notes while translating? What form are they in?
- Does the class work turn into the finished work, or are they separate?

**K2. ★ When you have a question, what happens to it? Trace one from the
moment it appears in your head.**
The cost of asking is the single largest hidden expense in this work, and
tracing one question exposes every step of it without requiring anyone to
characterise themselves.
- How long does a question sit before you ask it? How do you reach the
  person, and how long until an answer?
- What do you do with the passage while you're waiting?
- How many are waiting right now? How many die unasked in a month?

**K3. What kinds of question do you feel fine asking, and what kinds do you
not?**
Names the shame boundary directly, which tells me exactly which lookups the
tool must answer silently so that no person ever has to be asked.
- Is it different asking Geshe Michael versus a fellow student?
- Has anyone ever made you feel bad for asking something?
- What would you rather a machine answered so you never had to ask a person
  at all?

**K4. ★ When he answers a terminology question — or corrects something of
yours — what happens to the answer afterwards? Where does it live?**
His rulings are the highest-value data in the entire project and are almost
certainly evaporating into notebooks, memories and inboxes. Learning where
they currently die is more useful than any feature discussion.
- Did you write it down at the time? Could you find one he gave six months
  ago?
- Do the others get the answer, or only whoever asked?
- Would you want those collected somewhere everyone could see, if he
  approved it?

**K5. Do you and the other translators compare terms? How?**
Team consistency is either a real practice or a shared fiction, and I need
to know which before designing for it.
- Who resolves it when two of you disagree?
- Is there a shared list, and is it current? Would you want one?

**K6. Which of his materials do you keep beside you while you work?**
Identifies the physically present authority — a printed glossary, a course
book, a stack of transcripts — which is the thing any digital version must
be better than, not merely equal to.
- Is it marked up? May I see how?
- What do you look up in it, and how often?
- Is there anything in it you've stopped trusting or found out of date?

**K7. Is there anything about how the team works that you've never quite
understood and haven't asked?**
The process-shame question. People don't know the conventions, assume
everyone else does, and quietly guess for years.
- How long have you been not asking it?
- Is it written down anywhere you could have looked?
- What else do you wish were written down somewhere?

### L. Your system, and where a tool must not come

**L1. ★ You've arrived at a way of working. What's the best part of it —
the bit I'd be a fool to disturb?**
Names the untouchable explicitly, so it can be protected by design rather
than discovered by breaking it.
- How long did it take you to arrive at that?
- Has anyone tried to change it before? What happened?

**L2. ★ Where in all of this should a computer never appear? Be blunt with
me.**
The most valuable question in the guide. A map of forbidden ground is a
deliverable in its own right, and asking for it directly signals that my
goal is fit rather than conversion. Write the answer verbatim and do not
soften it.
- Is that about the machine itself, or about what it might do?
- Is there a version that would be all right — off to one side, or only
  when you ask?
- Would your answer be different for someone starting out?

**L3. Tell me about the last time you did change how you work. What made it
worth it?**
Switching history is evidence; a hypothetical answer about willingness to
change is not.
- What did it cost you in the first week? What nearly made you go back?
- Did you go back?

**L4. What would something new have to do before you'd trust it with a real
passage on a deadline?**
Gets the adoption bar stated by the person who holds it, in their terms,
before they have seen anything to react to.
- Would you run it alongside your own way first? For how long?
- What would make you stop mid-trial?

**L5. What would make you quit in the first ten minutes — and what would a
tool have to do, once, for you to stop using it forever?**
Disqualifiers are far cheaper to learn now than after release, and people
name them instantly. For someone with a scar the answer will be a specific
behaviour, not a vague quality.
- Has that already happened to you with something else? Which one?
- Would an apology or a fix bring you back, or is it final?
- What would make you feel stupid using it? What would make you hide that
  you were using it?

**L6. If it made you faster but you learned less, would you want it?**
The central ethical question for this tool. The answer decides which helps
default to on, which stay hidden until asked for, and whether there is a
try-first mode.
- Which parts would you want to still have to do yourself?
- Would you want a mode that made you attempt it before showing you
  anything?
- What did you learn by struggling that you're glad you struggled through?

**L7. If a tool saved you two hours a day but changed the way you read the
page, what would you say?**
Forces the trade-off every efficiency argument hides, and tells me whether
time is even the currency this person deals in — which software usually
assumes without checking.
- What would you do with the two hours?
- Is there a part of this you wouldn't want to go faster?
- Would you want a tool for the dull parts and no tool for the rest?

**L8. Would you rather it made you look competent to other people, or
actually helped you when nobody's watching?**
Surfaces the social dimension, which is a real requirement — private mode,
no visible history — and is essentially never volunteered.
- Would you mind if anyone could see what you'd looked up?
- Would you use it during a class, in front of people?
- Would you want your lookups saved at all?

### M. Three wishes — ask BEFORE showing anything

**M1. ★ A genie grants you three things for this work, ready tomorrow. They
don't have to be software, and they don't have to be reasonable. Be greedy
and be specific.**
Must be asked before any demonstration; wishes collected afterwards are
echoes of what I showed and are worthless as evidence. Letting them be
non-software is what makes this worth asking — *a typist*, *better light*,
*a photocopy of a dictionary I can't afford*, *six more hours a week* are
real answers, and some I can simply grant.
- What would that give you that you don't have now? What would it save you
  in a week?
- What does it look like on screen — or on the desk?
- Who else would want it?

**M2. ★ Now the mean version: you only get one. Which?**
Forced ranking exposes the true priority; three wishes always come out
roughly equal and therefore tell me nothing.
- Why that one? What would you give up to have it?
- And if I could only build one thing and it took me six months — still
  that one?

**M3. Now the smallest, stupidest thing. Something not worth mentioning
that would make tomorrow slightly easier.**
People offer big respectable wishes and hide the small real ones. The small
ones are usually cheap, high-frequency, and the actual difference between
using the tool and not.
- How many times a day does that thing happen, and what does it cost each
  time?
- Has anyone else ever complained about it, or do you assume it's just you?

**M4. And a wish in reverse: something you'd want taken away rather than
added.**
Subtraction is easier to name honestly than addition, and it reveals
burdens people have stopped complaining about. It also gives a reluctant
person a wish they can make with a clear conscience.
- How much of your week is that?
- Could anyone else do it, or does it have to be you?

**M5. One more, a selfish one: something that would make the work more
pleasant rather than faster.**
People stay at this for the pleasure of it. Dignity and delight never
appear on a requirements list and are often cheap to build.
- What's the nicest part of your setup as it is? What's the ugliest?

### N. After I've shown them anything — the last stretch

**N1. Of what you've just seen, which one thing — if it worked perfectly —
would be worth the disruption of changing how you work?**
Forces a single ranked answer instead of polite approval of everything, and
ties it to the real cost of switching.
- What would it have to get right before you'd rely on it?
- What would you still do by hand anyway?
- What's the first thing you'd test to see if it was lying to you?

**N2. What would you turn off?**
Subtraction is the currency of the release audit, and translators are
better at it than I am.
- Why that one? Hidden, or gone entirely?
- Would a beginner want it even if you don't?

**N3. What did you expect to see that wasn't there?**
Asks for a list rather than a compliment. *What do you think?* gets me
kindness; *what's missing?* gets me work.

**N4. Is there anything here you'd be uncomfortable with a student using?**
Casts them as guardian of the tradition rather than as a customer, and
surfaces honesty and pedagogy risks I cannot see from the inside.
- What would you want it to say instead? What should it refuse to do?
- What would you want a beginner to be forced to do by hand?

### O. Closing

**O1. What haven't I asked that I should have?**
The best material often arrives after the person has decided the interview
is over and the guard has come down. Leave a long silence after it.
- Is there something you assumed I already knew?
- Anything you nearly said earlier and didn't?
- Anything you'd want me to ask the others?

**O2. If someone you respect asked you today whether they should use this
thing, what would you tell them?**
A projection question that gets past personal politeness by letting them
advise a third party. It is also the honest measure of the hour: a hedge
means the tool has not earned this person yet, and that should be recorded
plainly rather than explained away.
- What would you warn them about?
- What would you need to see before you'd say yes?
- Would your answer be different for a beginner than for you?

**O3. Would you want to hear from me again about this — and if so, how? A
letter, a phone call, or not at all?**
Hands them control of the relationship and makes *not at all* an easy,
unembarrassing choice. Asking permission for the next contact is the
clearest proof the hour was not a recruitment exercise, and it is the last
impression they keep.
- Would you want to see what I write up from today?
- Is there anything from today you'd rather I didn't write down?
- May I quote any of this — and if so, with your name, or not?

## What to watch for

The answers are self-report; these are evidence. Note them in the margin
and ask about them afterwards, never in the moment.

**Timing and attention**

- **The first ninety seconds.** Whatever they do before they consider
  themselves to have started is the setup ritual, and they will not report
  it. Write the sequence down in order.
- **The dead air at the start of a line.** Time it silently. How many
  seconds between a new sentence appearing and the first English word being
  typed? It is usually enormous, and the sitting will be reported as
  continuous work.
- **The silences while working.** Long pauses are the tacit work happening.
  Mark the time, say nothing, and ask about that exact moment afterwards.
  Interrupting a pause destroys the thing I came for.
- **Their speed on the easy stretches.** Anything the tool adds to a fluent
  stretch is a net loss, however good it is at the hard bits.

**What their hands and eyes do**

- **Where the eyes and cursor go first** on a new sentence — left to right,
  or a sweep to the end for the verb. The clearest tell of whether
  sentence-shape reading has formed, and it cannot be asked for.
- **The home window.** The one they return to between every other action is
  the one a tool would have to replace or live beside — and it is often not
  the one they named as most important.
- **Reported versus observed references.** They will name one set of
  dictionaries and use another. Note the mismatch; do not point it out, or
  I will get a justification instead of the truth.
- **The named book versus the touched book**, and distance from the writing
  hand. Distance is a more honest frequency count than anything they can
  tell me.
- **Retyping instead of copying.** Any Tibetan typed by hand that already
  existed as text is pure mechanical loss and a place errors enter. Count
  the occurrences.
- **Doubled actions** — the same term searched in two places, the same
  thing checked twice. Each is a missing feature or a trust problem; note
  which.
- **Copy-paste chains.** Dictionary to note to draft. Count the hops; the
  chain length is a feature specification.
- **Mouse or keyboard.** A keyboard-first translator will abandon a
  mouse-first application within a day, whatever it does.
- **Reading aloud, or lips moving.** It happens at the hard bits, and it
  means sound is load-bearing rather than a nicety.

**Silences, skips and tells**

- **Silent skips.** They scroll past something without comment. Every silent
  skip is a stuck point that will never appear in any answer. Note the
  line, come back later: *"you went past that bit quite fast — what was
  happening there?"*
- **Failed searches, character by character.** What they type, edit, and
  retype, and what they conclude from zero results. Watch for the moment
  *it's not in there* follows a misspelling — that is the tax in its purest
  form, and they will never report it because they don't know it happened.
- **Scrolling back.** Re-reading something already translated means a
  decision made ten minutes ago has been lost with no way to pin it.
- **Hedge words in speech that don't survive into the draft** — *I think*,
  *probably*, *something like*. Count them, then check whether any of that
  uncertainty reached the page. The gap is the size of the problem.
- **The pause before "that's fine."** A genuine yes comes fast and brings a
  detail with it (*"yes — I'd use that on Thursdays for the class
  handout"*). A polite yes comes after a beat and stays abstract. Note the
  length of the pause, not the words.
- **The softener "for me."** *That wouldn't work for me*, *I'm probably not
  the right person*, *others would find it useful* — all three usually mean
  *that is wrong*, with the edge filed off. Treat each as a full objection.
- **Whether they look at my face before answering.** An answer delivered to
  my face, checking for approval, is worth nothing. If they start checking,
  I am leading — look down at the notebook and stop making eye contact for
  a while.
- **What happens when I stay silent for five seconds.** The first answer is
  the courteous one; the second is the true one. Most of what matters lives
  in that second answer.

**Objects and improvisations**

- **Homemade tools** — a taped index card, a colour code, a hand-drawn
  table, a slip of paper in the back of a book. Every one is a feature
  specification written by the person who needed it, and none will be
  mentioned voluntarily, because to them it is not a tool, it is just how
  they do it.
- **Whether the glossary was already open.** Open before I asked means it is
  genuinely part of the work; opened because I asked means it is an
  archive. That distinction changes everything built for it.
- **Pages they skip past quickly in a notebook.** Something there is
  private, unfinished or embarrassing. Do not push, do not look — note that
  the notebook has zones. It tells me a shared or synced version would be
  refused, and why.
- **What is written on paper.** Handwritten notes are decisions that never
  reached any file.
- **Where they park something they're about to lose** — a scratch line at
  the bottom of the document, a note app, a message to themselves. That is
  the capture surface the tool has to beat.
- **What they do with a wrong answer** — close the window, or leave it open
  just in case. Leaving it open means they don't fully trust their own
  first judgement, which is where evidence helps.

**Physical and emotional facts nobody mentions**

- **Zooming, squinting, leaning in, enlarging the font.** Legibility of
  Tibetan at their real settings on their real screen is a hard
  requirement. If they lean toward the screen, that is a finding about
  typography, not about them.
- **Glasses, a magnifier, a lamp pulled close, a very small screen, a bad
  chair.** If reading a woodblock scan on that screen would be physically
  hard, they will not say so — they will simply never use that pane, and be
  polite about it.
- **Phone use they don't count as software** — photographing a page, a
  dictionary app, messaging someone a line, a voice note. Someone who says
  *I don't use computers for this* may be doing four digital things an
  hour. Ask lightly, never as a gotcha.
- **Who else is implied in the sentences** — *we*, *they typed it up*, *she
  sends it back*, *someone printed it for me*. Invisible collaborators are
  often the real point of contact for any tool, and they are never on the
  invitation list.
- **Apologies.** *Sorry, this is a mess*, *I know this is silly*, *you'll
  think this is primitive*. Each one marks a workaround normalised years
  ago, or someone expecting to be sold something. Follow every apology —
  and correct the second kind sincerely and at once, or the rest of the
  hour will be defensive.
- **Nervous laughter and "this is embarrassing."** Every one is a feature
  request wearing a disguise. Mark the moment; do not reassure; come back
  gently later.
- **"I'll fix that later."** A deferred-work list that lives nowhere. Ask at
  the end how many they can still remember.
- **"I don't know why I do that."** Tacit knowledge surfacing. Mark it and
  come back; do not let it pass.
- **How they react when something goes wrong on screen.** A person who says
  *sorry, I always do this* when software misbehaves has been absorbing
  tool failures as personal failures for years.
- **Tone shift when Geshe Michael or the published translations come up.**
  Care, hesitation, a change of register. Do not push into it; note it and
  let them lead.
- **Time claimed versus time observed.** If they perform a lookup during the
  session, time it quietly. Tedious steps are routinely under-reported by
  half. The discrepancy, not the estimate, is the finding.

## What to show, and when

**Nothing for the first forty minutes.** My screen stays dark; the laptop
stays in the bag, then closed on the table. The moment the tool appears, a
witness becomes a guest and everything after that is politeness. **If the
hour runs out and I never show anything, the session succeeded.** If time
is short, cut the showing entirely — the watching is worth more.

**Ask permission twice, and mean the second one.** *"Would it be useful if I
showed you one thing, or would you rather I didn't?"* A hesitation is a no:
*"let's not — I'd rather use the time on you."* They are testing whether the
promise made in minute three was real, and passing that test is worth more
than anything on the screen.

**Open with a caveat, not a feature, and with permission to be rude.** Name
something the tool does badly or does not do at all. Then: *"what I need
from you is three things that are wrong with these. If you tell me it's
nice I'll have learned nothing. 'I wouldn't use that' is the most useful
sentence you can say for the next ten minutes."*

**Hand over the keyboard. I do not drive.** One sentence about what the
thing is for, then silence. Watching someone fail to find the button is the
finding; a smooth guided tour is not. But never *"here, you try it"* to
someone reluctant — that turns a conversation into a test they can fail in
front of a stranger. If they reach for it, go quiet and let them get lost.

**At most three things, all on their own material.** Never a canned demo
text. Their own hard word from twenty minutes ago reads as an answer; a
prepared example reads as a sales pitch.

1. **Their own stuck word or passage from earlier.** Click a word; let the
   longest phrase the dictionary knows light up; show Geshe Michael's own
   English for it, how often each rendering is attested, and the corpus
   passages that attest it — provisional ones visibly provisional, nothing
   composed. Say *"you spent four minutes on this earlier — here's what it
   would have shown you"*, then be quiet and let them judge it. This is the
   most persuasive thing available and it costs no promises.
2. **The honesty marks — and show it refusing before showing it
   succeeding.** Feed it something it cannot handle and let them watch it
   flag rather than guess: the dashed amber underline on a provisional
   gloss, the red wave on a syllable that isn't legal Tibetan, the bracketed
   marker where a conversion failed, the blank that stays blank. For anyone
   burned by a tool that guessed, watching a machine decline to answer is
   more persuasive than any correct answer. Then say once, quietly,
   *"nothing here was invented by the machine"*, and let them check it. Ask
   directly whether the marks reassure or look like clutter.
3. **Whichever of their three wishes already exists** — and ask *"is that
   the thing you meant?"* Often it will not quite be, and the gap between
   their wish and my build is the most valuable finding of the session.

**Two things to show only if the matching failure actually happened in the
session:** looking a word up by how it sounds, for the person who can hear a
term but not spell it; and the affixed-form search that finds the base word,
for the person whose search came back empty. Otherwise they are noise.

**Pull the network cable, or turn the wifi off, in front of them, and keep
working.** Dictionary, corpus, search and conversions all run on their own
disk, no account, no reporting. Thirty seconds of that replaces a page of
privacy assurances.

**Disclose the AI feature myself, before they find it** — where it is, that
nothing happens unless a button is pressed, and that its output is
banner-labelled as machine-written. Then ask the honest question: *"would
you use this instead of doing the work? Would you tell anyone you had?"*

**Show the way back to paper.** A passage printed or laid out as a page they
could work on with a pen. A tool that ends on paper fits their life; one
that only ends on a screen is asking them to move house.

**Do not show:** the menus, the pane inventory, the feature count, the
cataloguing workflow, the input-centre workstation, the alignment authoring
tool, the edition-comparison suite, the release machinery, the trainer and
drills, the OCR — anything they gave no sign of needing. Volume reads as
*this is not for me*, and a tour is a sales presentation with a mouse. One
sentence at the end if they ask what else is in there.

**Never open their glossary in the tool.** Do not import it, do not offer to
convert it, do not show what it would look like inside. Ask instead whether
I may study a copy afterwards, on my own time, and say what for.

**Do not show a feature to answer a complaint.** If they say something is
hard and my hand moves toward the mouse, that is the demo reflex firing. The
correct response is *"tell me more about that one."* The feature can go in
the follow-up email.

**Stop the moment interest fades.** If the shoulders drop or the answers go
flat, close the laptop mid-sentence: *"that's enough of that."* Then write
down, without judgement, that they were not interested. Stopping early is
what makes the next conversation possible.

**Close the showing with "what's missing here?"** — not *"what do you
think?"* The first gets a list; the second gets a compliment.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Leading.** *Wouldn't it be useful if…*, *I imagine that's frustrating?*,
  *so you'd want X, right?* People agree, warmly, and I go home with a tool
  specified by my own guesses in their voice. Never put the feature inside
  the question: *how do you keep track of terms?*, not *would a terminology
  checker help?* If I have already named the feature, the answer is
  contaminated and should be marked as such in my notes.
- **Defending.** The instant I explain why something is the way it is,
  criticism stops for the rest of the session — and permanently, because
  they now know what feedback costs. The only permitted responses are
  *thank you*, *that's fair*, and *tell me more*. Corrections, if genuinely
  needed, go in the written follow-up a week later.
- **Promising.** *I'll build that this week* creates a debt I may not pay,
  after which honest feedback is gone. Say instead: *"that's written down. I
  don't know yet whether it will be built — but you'll hear from me either
  way."* One caught overpromise silently discounts everything else I say.
- **Teaching, or correcting their translation.** The strongest temptation of
  the hour, and it re-establishes the status gap I spent five minutes
  dismantling. Never — especially when I am right. If asked directly:
  *"I honestly want to see what you'd do without me — ask me again at the
  end."*
- **Any sentence beginning "why didn't you just…"** The deadliest phrase
  available. It converts a workflow observation into a verdict on their
  competence, and there is no recovery from it inside the same session.
- **Letting it become a demo.** If I am talking more than a quarter of the
  hour, or the tool appeared before minute forty, this is a sales call and
  the data is contaminated. Better to end fifteen minutes early having only
  watched.
- **Filling silence.** Five seconds feels unbearable and is exactly where
  the honest sentence lives. Count to seven. The best material arrives after
  a pause I did not fill.
- **Reassuring after an admission.** *Oh, everyone finds that hard!* closes
  the topic and reads as an offer to move on. Follow admissions with
  curiosity, not comfort.
- **Praising them.** *You're doing great* is patronising from someone in a
  position of authority and pushes them into performance. Praise the
  information instead: *"that's exactly the kind of thing I can't get from
  anyone else."*
- **Banking a soft yes.** *That's interesting*, *that could be useful*, *I'm
  sure others would want that* are all noes. Convert or discard: *"when
  would you have used that — this week?"* If no occasion can be named, write
  it in the notes as a no, not a maybe. A folder of maybes is how a tool
  ends up full of things nobody uses.
- **Asking "do you like it?"** Likes are free and mean nothing. Ask *"would
  you use this on Tuesday, on the passage that's due?"* — a question that
  costs something to answer yes to.
- **Treating "I don't want it" as an objection to overcome.** It is a
  finding, and it may be the most valuable sentence of the day. The moment I
  start handling objections I am a salesman and the hour is lost.
- **Visible note-taking at the moment of a confession.** Typing hard the
  instant someone says *I've never understood particles* looks like a record
  being made of a failure. Keep notes uniformly light and write it up
  immediately afterwards.
- **Taking notes on a laptop with someone who works on paper.** A small
  insult delivered continuously for an hour. Pen, notebook, visible, offered
  for inspection.
- **Comparing them to anyone.** *The others said…*, *Nick does it this way*,
  *do you find it slower than most?* Not once, not even flatteringly.
  Comparison is the exact fear that brought them in guarded, and it turns a
  conversation into a ranking.
- **Invoking Geshe Michael to win a point.** *He liked it*, *he asked for
  this*, *he's been using it*. It converts a design question into a question
  of loyalty and ends honest disagreement instantly. His name belongs in
  this session only as the subject of their answers.
- **Arguing the house rules in the room.** If they disagree with a published
  rendering, or with the principle that his English is binding, listen,
  record it exactly, and thank them. Do not defend, do not negotiate, and do
  not report their disagreement as a request for a change.
- **Engineer language.** Pane, index, corpus, tier, provenance, spine,
  gloss, workflow, user, feature, adoption, onboarding, pain point, use
  case. Every one tells a non-engineer this was built for somebody else. Say
  *the page*, *search*, *Geshe Michael's own translations*, *marked as
  not-final*, *how you work*.
- **Naming features early.** Even *"we have a terminology checker"* in the
  first hour contaminates the wishes and the sticking-point answers. Keep
  the tool's vocabulary out of my mouth until minute forty.
- **Recruiting them in the session.** Do not ask, during the hour, whether
  they will test a build. Asking retroactively turns the whole session into
  the price of a favour, and a polite person will say yes and quietly not do
  it. Ask a week later, in writing, with an easy way to decline.
- **Photographing without asking each time.** One blanket permission at the
  start is not permission for the next page. Accept a no without visible
  disappointment.
- **Bringing anyone else.** Two visitors make an audience, an audience makes
  a performance, and a performance is all courtesy.
- **Overrunning.** Even if it is going wonderfully — especially then. Going
  past the announced end teaches them their stated boundaries are
  negotiable, and everything after that point is compliance rather than
  testimony. Ending on time buys the second session.
- **Treating a genie wish as a specification.** The wishes are a mood
  reading, not a build list. What people say they want and what the observed
  failures show they need diverge most sharply for the people who most need
  help.

## What to leave with

Written up the same day, from the notebook, before the next session:

- **Their day as steps, in their words**, with the format at every
  boundary — what arrives, what they do, what leaves, what they convert by
  hand in between, and where paper becomes typed and who does that typing.
- **Real numbers**: minutes to the first English word; lines or folios per
  sitting; the longest a single line has taken; how many questions are
  waiting unasked right now; how many parked passages are open. No survey
  will ever produce these.
- **The reach list, in the order the hand moves**, with rough distances, and
  the one they don't trust with their reason in their own phrasing.
- **The three to five things they do by hand in every single sitting.** This
  is the build list.
- **Every failed search, in the exact characters typed.** Wrong spellings,
  wrong script, the affixed form that returned nothing — a direct,
  unarguable specification for search tolerance, obtainable no other way.
- **The stuck-point list**: each place they halted, how long, and what they
  did about it — guessed, asked, skipped, gave up. Sorted by frequency, this
  is the feature priority list, and it will not match the three wishes.
- **The passage that beat them**, saved with permission as a permanent test
  fixture. Any build that cannot help with that specific passage has not
  helped this person.
- **Their homemade workarounds**, described or photographed with permission.
  The truest feature requests in the project, written by the person who
  needed them, and usually cheap to honour.
- **Permission to study a copy of their glossary offline**, with what each
  column means in their words. A no is itself a finding about trust; record
  it rather than pushing.
- **The scar story, precisely**: what guessed or changed something, when
  they noticed, what it cost to fix, and — the important part — what the
  program would have had to do instead for them to have forgiven it. That
  last clause is a testable requirement and belongs beside rules 1 and 3.
- **The "must not" list**: where a tool may not appear, what would make them
  abandon it in one stroke, and the untouchable part of their system,
  written down so a future design decision cannot quietly break it.
- **Their checks in two columns** — the ones a machine could do (they
  already believe in them and skip them for tedium: pre-approved work) and
  the ones a machine must never do (a boundary).
- **Their personal error signature**, the seed of a personal checklist
  rather than a generic one.
- **The shame map**: which questions they feel fine asking a person and
  which they don't. Everything on the *don't* side is a lookup the tool must
  answer silently, instantly, and without a record a third party could see.
- **Their answer on blanks and guesses** (F9) and on machine-composed
  English (F10), verbatim. These are the honesty rules in a translator's own
  words.
- **The three wishes ranked, the smallest stupidest thing, and the wish in
  reverse — all verbatim**, recorded before they saw anything. Paraphrase
  always drifts toward what I already planned to build.
- **Their answer to the crutch question** (faster but learning less), the
  adoption bar, and the ten-minute disqualifiers, quoted rather than
  summarised.
- **The list of what they would turn off**, with reasons — straight into the
  audit. A cut list is worth more than any feature request.
- **A clear consent record**: what may be quoted, whether their name may
  ever be attached (default no), and what happens to any recording and when
  it is deleted.
- **An honest internal verdict, written the same day: does this person need
  this tool at all?** If no, say so and defend it, and record what they *do*
  need instead — a typist, a lamp, a photocopy, an introduction, or simply
  to be left alone.
- **One named thing I will cut or change because of this hour**, decided
  before I leave and written at the top of the page. If the session produced
  no cut, either I led the witness or I was not listening, and it should be
  reconsidered rather than filed as a success.

## What happens afterwards

1. **The same day**: type up the notes, anonymised, and write the internal
   verdict and the one named cut. File the numbers into the audit evidence
   per pane; file the stuck-point list into the priority list.
2. **Within a week**: send them a one-page write-up in plain prose — what I
   heard, in their order — with *"cross out anything I got wrong and send it
   back."* Nothing quoted under their name. With a long-serving translator
   this is the strongest trust-building act available, and their corrections
   will be better than my notes. Include the consent record in writing so
   they can see it was kept.
3. **Keep the one small promise, with its date.** Not a feature — the build
   and a one-page start guide by Friday, or the answer to that particle
   question by tomorrow. Willingness to be honest a second time is bought
   entirely with the first kept promise.
4. **Report the honest failure too.** If I took a stuck passage away, tell
   them what the tool did and did not manage with it, including that it
   found nothing. Following up with a failure is the strongest possible
   proof that the promises made in minute three were real.
5. **Offer the standing low-cost channel**: *"when you're stuck on a word,
   text me the word. No explanation, no context — I want the raw stream."*
   Six weeks of that will be worth more than the interview.
6. **Ask, in writing and a week later, about a second session** — thirty
   minutes, them translating with a build in hand, me silent and watching it
   get in the way. Make declining easy and explicit. Respect exactly what
   they said about further contact: letter, call, or nothing.
7. **After all nine**: write `docs/TRANSLATOR_WORKFLOWS.md` — the nine days
   as steps, the common spine and the genuine divergences — and a one-page
   *must not* register that every future design decision is checked
   against. Feed the cut lists into the release audit and the wishes into
   the roadmap, marked with how many people asked and how many merely
   approved.
8. **Send them the list of what changed because of them**, a few months on,
   one page. A translator who sees their objection actually cost the project
   something becomes, quietly, the project's best friend.
