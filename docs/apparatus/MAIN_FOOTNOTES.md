## A Song of My Spiritual Life (tr. Chang w/ GMR, 2025) (53 notes)

**n.1 — Je Tsongkapa**  
This is, incidentally, the correct way to write our author's name in English pronunciation. Other ways of doing it are confusing transcription with pronunciation.

**n.2 — The Hundred Thousand Songs**  
This is perhaps an overly-literal translation of the Tibetan word bum (technical spelling 'bum), which does mean “hundred thousand,” but traditionally simply refers to the collected writings of an author (as in gsung-'bum).

**n.3 — Abbreviated Presentation of the Steps to the Path**  
Please see the preface to this volume for more information about the title.

**n.4 — Sumati Kirti**  
This is the Sanskrit version of Je Tsongkapa's ordination name, which is “Lobsang Drakpa,” or “Pure-Minded One of Great Renown”; in the technical Tibetan, Blo-bzang grags-pa. The technical Sanskrit spelling is Sumati Kīrti.

**n.5 — High & holy Lama**  
In a common classical Tibetan literary device, the parts of a high lama's name are tucked into a verse of poetry. We have italicized these parts for the reader; in Tibetan, a small “x” mark would typically be carved under them.

**n.6 — Namo Guru Manjughoshaya**  
Which is to say, “I bow down to my Teacher, Manjushri”—the Buddha of Wisdom, whose name means “Gentle Voice.” The point is that emptiness constantly whispers its truth to us.

**n.7 — Causes for the signs & marks**  
For the reference in the String of Precious Jewels of Arya Nagarjuna (c. 200AD), see f. 113b (entry S2 in the attached bibliography of works originally written in Sanskrit, corresponding to catalog number TD04158 of the Derge edition of the Tengyur, or collection of classical Indian Buddhist commentaries).

**n.8 — Definite good**  
A code word for nirvana & enlightenment.

**n.9 — Widespread activities & profound view**  
“Widespread activities” is a term used for the activities of a bodhisattva—a person seeking to help every being in the wide, wide universe. “Profound view” refers to the understanding of emptiness which facilitates these activities.

**n.10 — Entire group of the jewels**  
This group is traditionally listed as six, with some variations between lists. A typical version would include the two “jewels of the middle way,” Arya Nagarjuna (c. 200AD) and his heart disciple, Master Aryadeva (c. 250AD); the two “jewels of higher knowledge,” Arya Asanga and his half-brother, Master Vasubandhu (c. 350AD); and the two “jewels of accurate perception,” Masters Dignaga (c. 440AD) and Dharmakirti (c. 650AD). This way of listing the six is from the Great Dictionary (B16, R00002); see the entry under rGyan drug in any of the various editions.

**n.11 — Two teaching traditions**  
“Lama of the Golden Isle” is a reference to Suvarna Dvipa Guru, whose personal name was Dharmakirti, not to be confused with the later master of logic and perceptual theory by the same name. He lived on the island of Sumatra c. 1000AD; a presentation about his life, and that of his contemporaries, may be found in the preface to the Diamond Cutter Classics publication Deathless Nectar for Helping Others: Notes to a Teaching on the “Crown of Knives” (bibliography entry E2 below). Vidya Kokila, an Indian master of the Middle Way school, is also dated to about 1000AD. It seems that his name should be correctly spelled in Tibetan as Rig-pa'i khu-byug, rather than Rigs-pa'i khu-byug.

**n.12 — Concluding section of the “Great Book”**  
Je Rinpoche does indeed speak specifically to this topic in the final piece of one of his longest & most famous classics. See the section at f. 519a beginning from Thub-pa'i gsung-rab rab-'byams (B8, S05392L).

**n.13 — Wishing jewel**  
The ancient Asian equivalent of an Aladdin's Lamp, this is a common gemstone found in a river; treated with sacred substances; and then prayed over at the top of a staff, on a special holy day—which imparts the power to grant its owner anything they wish for.

**n.14 — Greater and lesser ways**  
The “greater way” is spiritual practice motivated by an intention to serve, eventually, every living being in the universe. The “lesser way” is motivated, for the time being, by a desire to avoid a bad rebirth, and all the sufferings of a normal life. The second leads, in time, to the first.

**n.15 — Do your practice the same**  
These lines are indeed found in our text as it appears in the collection of briefer works, except that untypically Je Rinpoche has referred to himself there with the high honorific verb bgyis. See f. 56a (B10, S05275-59).

**n.16 — As may be appropriate**  
For a typical prayer-book use of the alternate lines, see for example f. 181b of A Compendium of Liturgical Texts Utilized at the Various Major Monasteries (B15, S00207). The point is that—when monastics or us modern people sit, especially together, and read Je Rinpoche's text out loud—these particular lines are not (as for Je Tsongkapa) a statement of what we have accomplished, but of what we hope to accomplish. Still, on an advanced level, seeing ourselves as him and reciting his words as he would himself itself represents a traditional, powerful practice of the steps of the path.

The most detailed and accessible source of the kind mentioned here is certainly A Gift of Liberation, Thrust into the Palm of Your Hand, by the great Pabongka Rinpoche, Dechen Nyingpo (1878-1941) (B12, S00004). The section mentioned begins at f. 66a of the Tibetan; an excellent English translation is available (E1), and the same section can be accessed by looking for the same Tibetan folio number inserted in the translation.

**n.20 — Freedom and omniscience**  
When freedom is presented this way, as the counterpart of omniscience or total Buddhahood, it refers to a kind of nirvana in which we have reached only our personal goals.

**n.21 — Three different viewpoints**  
These are covered in many of the more detailed presentations of the steps of the path. The Path of Bliss, for example, composed by His Holiness the First Panchen Lama, Lobsang Chukyi Gyeltsen (1565-1662), briefly describes the “cause” here as beginning with a prior practice of completely pure morality, accompanied by practices such as the perfection of giving, and then also immaculate prayers or intentions. As for essential nature, a fully functional human being is simply statistically extremely rare, among all the possible forms of life. Finally, the occurrence of a healthy, human body & mind can be compared to the metaphor of a star shining in broad daylight: impossible almost by definition. See the discussion beginning from f. 7b (B13, S05944).

**n.22 — These are the protection**  
See f. 251a of Master Chandrakirti's Seventy Lines on Going for Shelter to the Three (S1, TD03971).

**n.23 — Our taking shelter is steadfast**  
The English translation here is based on Je Rinpoche's original lines, with the relevant spelling of brtan-pa. Choney Lama has used the spelling bsten-pa, which has exactly the same pronunciation and fittingly conveys “relying completely.”

**n.24 — The holy Lamas**  
Referring to the alternate form of the last two lines here; see the commentary to verse 12 above.

**n.25 — The eight causes**  
The desired results that will help us with our practice are listed as follows by the great Jamyang Shepay Dorje, Ngawang Tsundru (1648-1721), in his Lineage Words of the Lama (f. 24a, B6, S19061): (1) a long life; (2) a beautiful complexion; (3) high social standing; (4) complete authority; (5) perfect credibility; (6) widely recognized strength; (7) respected personal qualities; and (8) a very healthy form.

**n.26 — Depending constantly**  
The peculiar preposition here in the Tibetan, rgyun tu, is a not uncommon variant for rgyun du which probably harkens back to an older secondary suffix letter da.

**n.27 — The four powers of purification**  
We find a detailed explanation of these four, of course, in Pabongka Rinpoche's Gift of Liberation (see the section starting at f. 111b, B12, S00004). (1) The “power of the basis” involves the practice of going for refuge, in actions such as re-committing ourselves to our daily meditation practice, or daily reading of scripture. (2) The “power of destruction” refers to feeling strong regret for our mistaken actions, and of course involves a strong understanding of all the bad things those karmas will inevitably bring us. (3) The “power of restraint” is described as the most powerful of the powers, and refers to making a commitment to avoid repeating our mistake. In a case where the mistaken behavior is especially chronic or easy to commit, we might want to set a doable time limit for not repeating the action: for example, not engaging in useless talk for the next 24 hours. (4) The “power of the antidote” refers to particularly effective opposite actions, such as (with useless talk) seeking out conversations with our fellow Dharma students about the topic of emptiness.

**n.28 — Find within ourselves**  
This phrase is how we have translated the Tibetan verb bsten-pa here, which literally means “to rely upon” things like a doctor or medicine. Many versions of the root text (such as our own, here) spell this word here with a homonym: brtan-pa. This word would indicate that our feelings of disgust and exhaustion with our current life situation should be very strong, or firm.

**n.29 — In the mindstream of a common being**  
The expression “common being” is used to refer to a person who has not yet perceived emptiness directly.

**n.30 — Ten negative actions**  
These begin with three actions that we commit physically: (1) taking life, (2) stealing others' things, and (3) sexual misconduct—here, primarily referring to adultery with another person's committed partner. Next are four bad deeds of speech: (4) lying; (5) splitting up other people with things that we say; (6) harsh types of speech; and (7) idle talk. Then finally are the three mental misdeeds of (8) being unhappy when something good happens to someone else; (9) being happy when something bad happens to them; and (10) wrong views such as the belief that the laws of karma are mistaken, or that there is no ultimate refuge.

**n.31 — As we find it in the greater way**  
It might seem superfluous to refer to the Wish “as found in the greater way”; but technically there do exist, in some classical presentations, wishes for goals lower than complete enlightenment for the sake of every living being in the universe.

**n.32 — Six types of giving**  
Not a terribly common division, but found in the Jewel of the Shining Gem, a commentary to the Compendium of the Sutras by Arya Nagarjuna, as giving which is (1) unconditional; (2) performed with joy; (3) repeated again & again; (4) presented to an appropriate recipient; (5) total; and (6) offered to ones followers. See f. 228a (S4, TD03935).

**n.33 — This jewel**  
The Tibetan for this word in the carving we are using is spelled rin-cen, which is a rare but acceptable variant for the nearly ubiquitous rin-chen.

**n.34 — Freedom from fear**  
This section is an indirect allusion to specific vows within the practice of advanced Buddhism, or the Diamond Way. Here, within the pledges of the “family” of the high Buddha known as the “Jewels,” we commit to four different forms of generosity: giving material things; giving protection; giving the Dharma; and giving love.

**n.35 — Same two topics**  
That is, first listing the fine qualities of the perfection, and then how to practice it.

**n.36 — Clears away the torment of the heat**  
In the traditional sources, rays of sunlight are described as functioning to ripen crops; while rays of moonlight serve to provide coolness to counteract excessive heat.

**n.37 — Central mountain of the world**  
Buddhist cosmology sees our planet as a flat disk with an incredibly high mountain at the center.

**n.38 — Skyglider of forbearance**  
Traditional Sanskrit, and Tibetan, poetics features vivid, made-up words as sort of riddles, to represent different objects and beings in the world. Here “palm walker” (in Sanskrit, bhujaṅga) refers to a snake, since it slides on its flat belly. And then “skyglider” (in Tibetan, nam-mkha' lding) connotes the garuda, a powerful mythical eagle much like the western phoenix, and which delights in carrying off snakes and eating them in mid-air. Your translators have observed this in person with the American eagle and rattlesnake, in the Arizona desert.

**n.39 — Three types of joyful effort**  
These three are the following. First we have the “armor-like” effort that we see here, where we are iron-plated in our determination to help all other beings. Then there is “joyful effort in the accumulation of goodness,” which is a joy in undertaking all the practices of the Dharma. And then finally we have “joyful effort in accomplishing the needs of others,” which includes for example the different traditional methods for attracting and serving our own disciples. For a great description, see as usual that of the great Pabongka Rinpoche and Trijang Rinpoche in A Gift of Liberation, starting from f. 344a (B12, S00004).

**n.40 — Never turn back**  
The spelling for this expression found in the commentary—mi bzlog—is an acceptable variant, and homonym (including the prenasal of the second syllable), for the spelling of the root text: mi ldog.

**n.41 — King of all mountains**  
Again, referring to that highest central mountain of our disk-like planet.

**n.42 — The Lord's “Steps on the Path”**  
Here referring to Je Tsongkapa's Great Book on the Steps of the Path to Enlightenment (the Lam Rim Chenmo); see the extremely detailed section beginning at f. 305b (B8, S05392L).

**n.43 — Just how things are**  
These three are listed for example by the great textbook writer of the glorious Sera Mey Tibetan Monastery, Kedrup Tenpa Dargye (1493-1568), as the wisdom with which we realize just how things are; the wisdom with which we perceive all the things there are; and the wisdom that accomplishes the needs of all living beings. See f. 127b of the first volume of his Jewel of the Essence of Good Explanation (B11, S00009-1).

**n.44 — Dissection Teaching**  
A “dissection teaching” refers to a commentarial style where the author points out concepts and practices as vividly as a medical instructor might lead their student doctors through the organs of an actual corpse laying on a table. For details of the work mentioned, see B2, S%%%%%.

It would appear that there are actually ten lines of relevant text here, even excluding the usual refrain.

**n.47 — Six lines**  
Again, not counting the refrain.

**n.48 — Individual understandings**  
It appears that the standard Tibetan spelling of the term that we have translated as “individual understandings” here is so-sor rtog-pa; the English translation is supported by the most common Sanskrit original, which is pratyavekṣa. And this is how we find the spelling in our root text. But we also frequently see Tibetan writers using so-sor rtogs-pa, as we do here in the commentary, which would tend more to mean “individual realizations,” or a more direct sort of perception. It seems that what is meant here is more the understanding.

**n.49 — Come into the care**  
Our commentator has mentioned only the opening line of each of the three verses mentioned, since they would be quite familiar to any monastic reading his work. For our reader's benefit, we have inserted the full prayer in each case. This first one is found in Je Tsongkapa's famous praise of the Buddha for teaching dependence from the point of view of emptiness (f. 15b, B9, S05275-15).

**n.50 — Reborn in the front row**  
We have excerpted this verse from a commentary to the famous “Thousand Angels of the Heaven of Bliss,” an oral tradition granted by Je Tsongkapa himself and first put into writing by Gyuchen Sangye Gyatso (born c. 1550), a teacher of His Holiness the First Panchen Lama, Lobsang Chukyi Gyeltsen (1565-1662). See f. 6a of A Dissection Presentation Related to the Lama Practice Called “The Thousand Angels of the Heaven of Bliss,” along with Private Instructions (B7, S19001).

**n.51 — The teachings should spread in this world**  
We've excerpted the full verse from f. 8a of Notes to a Teaching on the “Thousand Angels of the Heaven of Bliss,” a Lama Practice, written by Gungtang Konchok Tenpay Drunme (1762-1823) (B1, S00930).

**n.52 — Forever cleared away**  
As is traditional, the verse is filled with classical poeticisms that date back to ancient India. The friend of the night-blooming jasmine is the moon, whose rays coax its petals open. Moon & sun are both seen as moving across the sky mounted on a grand chariot drawn by massive mythical horses, called “racers.” The “road of the gods” is a reference to the sky.

**n.53 — Sustain the life of everlasting joy**  
This entire verse is a reference to an ancient Indian myth known as Samudra Manthana: the churning of an ocean of milk by the gods—using the central mountain of the world as a churning stick—to produce the nectar of immortality.

## The Other Side of Emptiness (tr. Chang w/ GMR, 2026) (28 notes)

**n.1 — Tashi Gepel Monastery**  
Ultimately a source for the name of the famous Rashi Gempil Ling temple and monastery in New Jersey, USA.

**n.2 — Pratītya**  
This part is a faux Sanskrit title that was quite corrupt in the original woodcarving; we have repaired it as well as we could, to match its Tibetan translation following. Whenever your translators correct a questionable reading in the original manuscript, we enclose it in curly braces and use a percentage sign (%)—as you see here.

**n.3 — Twin guides**  
A traditional poeticism for our two eyes.

**n.4 — Lion of the Shakyas**  
The historical Buddha was the leader of his clan, known as the Shakyas. Thus for example the way he is called Shakyamuni: Lord of the Shakyas.

**n.5 — Union of appearance and emptiness**  
“Appearance” here refers to the way in which the things we see in the world are actually composites of luminous images emerging from karmic seeds within our own mind; this is in fact why they are referred to as “dependent events,” as they rely on those seeds, planted when we were good or bad to other people. “Emptiness” then is the fact that there is nothing in the universe which does not come about in this same manner.

**n.6 — Midnight in the Land of Snows**  
It's a tradition in these opening verses to add many allusions and poetic images. The “Land of Snows” is, of course, Tibet; also referred to here as “the northern lands.”

**n.7 — Very heart of well-spoken expression**  
This phrase is part of the original title of Je Tsongkapa's praise of Lord Buddha for teaching dependent events, and significantly is also part of the title of Je Rinpoche's very famous work on interpreting the Buddha: The Essence of Eloquence: A Classical Commentary on the Topic of Distinguishing the Figurative from the Literal, in Lord Buddha's Teachings. The name of our Buddha's father translates as “Pure Repast.”

**n.8 — King of the Dharma**  
See bibliography entry %%E2, below.

**n.9 — River Schools**  
This was a catch-all term for non-Buddhist schools in ancient India, many of which believed that one could achieve salvation by sitting on a special platform slightly submerged in the Ganges River. Several typical “teachers” of these groups are named here. Those who would like further detail here can refer to the Diamond Cutter Classics publication Great Ideas of the East: A Survey of 101 Enlightening Belief Systems from Ancient Asia (%%E1).

**n.10 — Wonder and shame**  
We very frequently see—and most classically in the Treasure House of Higher Knowledge by Master Vasubandhu (c. 350AD) (see f. 5a, %%S1, TD04089)—the dichotomy of ethical behavior into that instigated by our own conscience, and that inspired by a concern for how others will view us. The former, in Tibetan, is called ngo-tsa (technical spelling the same); while the latter is trel-yu (spelled khrel-yod).

**n.11 — Rite of the Five Fires**  
A ritual among certain non-Buddhists of ancient India, where a person sits between a number of large fires, tortured by the heat as some kind of personal purification. It basically represents any kind of practice where we purposely damage our body or mind, with the idea that it benefits us in some way spiritually.

**n.12 — They stand**  
Manuscripts of the original ode have a various reading here, either lags or legs. The meaning remains the same.

**n.13 — Pale Yellow and such**  
A reference to a famous non-Buddhist teacher of olden times, whose name in Sanskrit is Kapila.

**n.14 — Three realms of existence**  
Our world—and in fact the universe composed of all such worlds—is said in Buddhism to be divided into three discrete realms. We live in the desire realm, distinguished by our desire for food and sex. Beyond our level is the form realm, populated by beings of exquisite bodies and possessions who typically drop to the hells after they die, as their fine karma is exhausted. And then there is the formless realm, inhabited by suffering beings of a very subtle existence almost beyond the physical.

**n.15 — Countless eons**  
The “countless” here can also refer to a specific number from ancient times; say, ten to the sixtieth power of eons (a decillion).

**n.16 — Expressed in the one verse**  
It seems that, rather, two verses are referenced here—although the point may be that we are to read them together.

**n.17 — The five high qualities**  
Je Tsongkapa himself, in his Highway for Bodhisattvas, describes these five as words which are (1) deep, like the thunder roar of a dragon; (2) sweet and melodius, like a beautiful song; (3) heart-stealing, pleasing; (4) perfectly logical, in their clarity; and (5) worthy of our attention, since they contain no contradiction.

**n.18 — Cooling light of the moon**  
In ancient times in Asia, the light of the sun was perceived as hot enough to ripen crops; while the light of the moon was cool enough to reduce heat or pain.

**n.19 — That glorious spreading Moon**  
The “moon” here is chandra in the Sanskrit, and thus represents Master Chandrakirti, who clarified the meaning of Arya Nagarjuna's works in works like Entering the Middle Way and A Clarification of the Verses, where “clarification” utilizes the same native word as our “light” here. Both of these classics shed light on The Root Text on Wisdom, by Arya Nagarjuna, itself that garden of night-blooming jasmine, releasing its scent only when struck by the light from this waxing disk of the moon.

**n.20 — Only to this one**  
Remember that “only this” (de kho-na) is also a code word for emptiness: the flip side of the dependence coin.

**n.21 — King of the Shakya People**  
Lord Buddha was originally a member of the royalty in a people of India known as the Shakyas.

**n.22 — Young moon in the sky**  
“Moon announced” is the literal meaning of the name of the great emptiness teacher Master Chandrakirti. A “young” moon is the crescent moon; and the great Hindu god of creation & destruction—Shiva—holds one at the crown of his head. Here we are picturing Je Tsongkapa, “Lobsang” Drakpa, with this same power.

**n.23 — Those fortunate ones**  
The thousand-petalled chakra—pictured as a lotus—in the body is located in the head, connected with the intellect. The “older brother” here is Gyalwa Gendun Gyatso, His Holiness the Second Dalai Lama, senior continuation of the First, a disciple of Je Tsongkapa. The Second's name literally means “ocean of aspiration for goodness,” and he “released” the meaning of Je Rinpoche's eulogy in a work entitled The Ultimate Point of Entry. This and the resulting lineage has allowed us lucky “bees” (bhramara in Sanskrit) to enjoy this deathless nectar.

**n.24 — Dragons of the sea**  
It is said that there are eight great dragon leaders spread throughout the seas of our planet; and so they have come to represent the number 8. Really perfect water—like the kind you'd want to have if you were offering water bowls on your altar—is said to have 8 different qualities: the water is (1) cool to the touch; (2) it is delicious to taste; (3) it is soft; (4) it is crystal-clear; (5) it does no harm to the throat; (6) it does no harm to the stomach; (7) it is free of any noxious smell, and (8) it is light.

**n.25 — Daughter of the Golden Womb**  
A reference to Sarasvati, the traditional Indian goddess of song and poetics. The entire verse is a reference to another ancient Indian belief that the world was created when the gods churned the milk of a timeless sea. The “King of Camphor” is a reference to the moon, again used as a crown to represent high intelligence.

**n.26 — The “Swan of Gentle Voice”**  
This is a sometimes-seen name which seems to relate to Lady Sarasvati (at times the consort of Gentle Voice, or Manjushri, the Buddha of Wisdom), as a reference to her lyrical skills; it is sometimes used for His Holiness the Second Dalai Lama, Gendun Gyatso (1507-1554), who has also written a commentary—entitled A Matchless Point of Entry—to Je Tsongkapa's classic on dependence. Chok-Hla Wuser (1429-1500) was an eminent scholar from Ratu Monastery who is best known for his work on the collected topics of logic; a very rare copy has been published by the Asian Classics Input Project. He is referenced in a minor writing of His Holiness Gendun Gyatso.

**n.27 — Sudharmeshvara**  
As his life went on, our author came to be associated with Sanskrit personal names—the primary one being Dharma Bhadra, or “Fine Dharma.” Here we see a Sanskrit translation of his novice ordination name, Wangchuk Chusang.

**n.28 — Year of Bliss**  
This is a nickname for the Wood Tiger year, which would have fallen in 1794. Note that Tibetan and Mongol years don't quite match up with western ones, but here we are close.

**n.60 — The Realized One, Nagarjuna**  
We are using “realized one” as a translation of the Sanskrit word arya, which refers to anyone who has experienced the direct realization of emptiness: one of the most crucial steps for later achieving nirvana and Buddhahood. Only a handful of realized ones are classically recognized in scripture, and Master Nagarjuna is perhaps the most prominent. **n.74 — Seventy Verses**

**n.75 — The Root Text on Wisdom**

**n.76 — The Commentary**  
See ff. 2a-2b (S8, TD03864) of Master Chandrakirti's Commentary to the Sixty Verses on Reasoning. Here, Master Chandrakirti explains that Seventy Verses on Emptiness and Putting an End to All Argument are extensions of The Middle Way (another name for Nagarjuna's Root Text on Wisdom)—and that, since they don't stand on their own, they don't have their own offerings of praise. He shows that Seventy Verses on Emptiness is a refutation of an idea put forth in The Middle Way, which starts with the lines: “Anything that grows, stays, or perishes is like an illusion, a dream, like a city of phantoms.” Putting an End to All Argument is a refutation of an idea in the work that begins with: “The nature of all things is that they lack causes. They are not things in & of themselves, nor by any means are they something else.”

**n.135 — Abstruse objects cannot be observed**  
This refers to a concept where all objects of perception are divided into three types: obvious ones; abstruse ones; and highly abstruse ones. Roughly, the red color of an apple would be an example of the first. A fire deep within a faraway mountain pass would be an example of the second, since it is “hidden”—in the sense that we can perceive it only through inference or deduction, based on the black color of smoke resulting from it which we can see directly. The subtle workings of the laws of karma are a traditional example of a highly abstruse object.

**n.162 — What the ultimate reasoning puts a stop to**  
Gyaltsab Je is being sarcastic, as he was wont to do. One is a reference to the impossible thing that emptiness is empty of; that is, anything which could exist without coming from our mental seeds (and which therefore could never exist in the first place). The other is a reference to all those (unfortunately certainly existing) negativities within ourselves that we seek to stop, by following the spiritual path. **n.250 — Shantarakshita and his spiritual son**  
Master Kamalashila—who wrote the Light on the Middle Way just mentioned, as well as an extraordinary commentary to the Diamond Cutter Sutra included in the present translation series—was the principal disciple of Master Shantarakshita, and is considered his spiritual son. The “Realized One” (Arya) mentioned here is Master Vimuktisena; while the “Lion” is a reference to Master Haribhadra (as hari means lion).

**n.251 — List of primary and ancillary positions**  
The following selection is found on ff. 114a-114b of his Overview of the Middle Way (B10, S00021).

**n.252 — Obstacle to nirvana**  
As opposed to the second classical type of obstacle: one to enlightenment itself.

**n.253 — Someone who believes that things are coming towards them**  
In Tibetan, tsur-mthong; where tsur means towards them, and mthong means to see. This is a uniquely descriptive technical term used to describe a person who has not yet seen emptiness directly. A “yogic direct perception” is explained by the eminent Jamyang Shepa as a meditative perception combining both perfect meditation (shamatha) and special insight into the nature of things (vipashyana). See ff. 390a-390b of his dialectic analysis of the middle way (B35, S13000).

**n.254 — Nirvana without anything left over**  
As opposed to the second type, which is nirvana “with something left over.” These are technical terms explained differently by different Buddhist schools.

**n.255 — “Correct” form of the deceptive**  
The idea that certain objects in deceptive reality (all of which “lie” to us) are more correct than others; that is, the idea that a rope which a normal person sees as a rope is less deceptive than a rope that they see as a snake—when the normal person is seeing both as coming from their own side, already.

**n.256 — Foundation wisdom**  
As opposed to “path wisdom” and omniscience; these three types of wisdom are emphasized by the Independent Group within the Middle Way School.

**n.257 — Still involved with the discrepancy**  
That is, where there is still a discrepancy between how objects appear to us, and how they actually exist.

**n.259 — Why do you say that**  
Kedrup Tenpa Dargye here drops into a style of monastic textbook composition where he and an imaginary opponent start to duel. Many of the traditional responses here are left unwritten, since they are assumed by a trained debater. We have supplied the opponent's responses, and put them in italics for easy identification by the reader.

**n.260 — His briefer commentary**  
A reference to the fact that Master Haribhadra composed two commentaries to the famed Jewel of Realizations of Maitreya. The first, called The Illumination, is 341 folios in the Tengyur; the second, simply called The Commentary, is 62.

**n.261 — Source of countless faults**  
An oft-repeated sentiment of the greatest minds of India and Tibet; its original source is Master Chandrakirti's “other” commentary on Arya Nagarjuna's Wisdom, entitled A Clarification of the Verses (see f. 8b, S7, TD03860).
