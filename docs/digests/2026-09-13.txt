# Diamond Cutter Translation Tool Daily Digest #6 --- Sunday, September 13 2026

**Headline.** I photographed pages from a Tibetan grammar textbook I own
and fed them into the work, and running the book's own example sentences
through our reader found five real faults in it --- including one where
the tool was confidently naming the commonest particle in the Tibetan
language as the verb of the sentence.

## What got done

### The reader learned three sentence shapes it had no name for

Classical Tibetan leaves things out far more often than English does,
and our tool had no way to say so.

**A verb can be used in a way that changes what everything around it
means.** A verb that normally takes a *doer* can be used in a
construction where it takes a *subject* instead --- the book calls it
attributive syntax, "A is called B". Our tool assumed a verb's type
settled the shape of its sentence. It does not. The tool now offers both
readings and says outright that it cannot tell which is meant.

**A Tibetan sentence can have no verb at all.** Two kinds. In the first,
"A is B", the word *is* is simply never written --- the book calls this
by far the most common sentence shape in this literature. In the second,
a sentence of existence or possession leaves out the word *has*. The
tool now recognises the shape of each and names it, while leaving the
reader the judgement English forces and Tibetan does not: whether
something *has* something, or *exists* somewhere.

### Five faults, found by running the book's own sentences

1.  **The tool was calling `la` the verb of the sentence.** `la` is the
    commonest particle in Tibetan --- closer to "to" or "at" than to any
    verb. Its dictionary entry carries 108 meanings, one of which is a
    pattern rather than a definition, and our tool accepted anything
    beginning with the word "to" as evidence of a verb. Across three
    thousand sample passages, `la` and one other particle were the two
    most frequently named "verbs" in the whole corpus.

2.  **It called verbs intransitive on no evidence.** Whenever a verb was
    absent from its reference tables, the tool announced the verb took
    no object --- and in the sentence I tested it, that was the opposite
    of the truth, printed directly underneath a line saying the sentence
    had a doer.

3.  **It cut Tibetan words in half.** Two words --- one meaning "visual
    consciousness", one meaning "the first" --- were being split down
    the middle, because part of each looks like a grammatical particle
    when read letter by letter. A sentence cannot end in the middle of a
    word, and now it doesn't.

4.  **It hunted for a verb where there was nowhere for one to be.** In
    27% of clauses the sentence structure leaves no slot for a verb at
    all. The tool searched anyway, and called what it found trustworthy
    in nearly half of those cases.

5.  **It named a verbal noun as the sentence's verb, confidently.** This
    is the construction the textbook singles out as the hard one, and
    the tool was getting it wrong with confidence rather than saying it
    was unsure.

### An idea of mine that measuring killed

I was about to teach the reader a rule: that a word ending in a
particular syllable is a noun, not a verb. Before changing anything I
measured how many real verbs take that ending. **The dictionary records
43% of its verbs in exactly that form.** The rule would have thrown away
nearly half the evidence the tool relies on. It is written down as a
dead end so nobody tries it again.

### A second opinion, from outside our tradition

Everything our tool knows about Tibetan grammar descends from one line
of teachers. That means its internal rules can agree with each other
perfectly and still be wrong together. Two grammars from a completely
different tradition --- 1883 and 1912, both long out of copyright ---
are now on the machine as an outside check. The older one immediately
confirmed a correction we had made the day before on the textbook's
authority alone.

### The reading page, rebuilt

The page had grown two blocks of statistics before any Tibetan appeared,
and a table that stated the same fact in three separate columns. It now
opens with two short lines, and the table says each thing once.

### A correction

I published a figure this morning saying the tool could not identify a
verb in 52% of the corpus. That was wrong. My sample had been filtered
by length rather than by whether the text was Tibetan, so it included
English headings and a fragment of a mantra. Measured properly, it is
**20%**. The wrong figure and how it happened are recorded beside the
right one.
