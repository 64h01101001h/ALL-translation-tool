# Diamond Cutter Translation Tool --- daily digest, Wednesday 9 September 2026 (afternoon, continuing)

**Headline.** This afternoon was almost entirely made of things I found
by using the tool rather than by planning to build them. I hovered over
a button and got nothing, so every button in the application now says

## Hovering over anything now tells you what it is

I hovered over the Library button and nothing appeared. It turned out
the pane switcher buttons carried no description at all, and twenty-six
of the buttons on the ribbon carried only their own name.

Both answer the same way now: the name in bold, then a sentence saying
what the thing is for. Fourteen of the pane descriptions are lifted word
for word from the user manual, so the tooltip and the manual cannot
drift apart over time.

A quieter problem sat underneath it. A ribbon button copied its
description once, at the moment it joined the ribbon, so a description
written any later was silently thrown away. That is the sort of thing
that makes a fix look like it did not work. It follows the source now,
and two tests hold the line: twenty-four panes and one hundred and
twenty-one buttons, none of them silent.

## Day now means Day

I had Day selected in Preferences and dark chrome on my screen. Both
were behaving exactly as written. The old switch only ever *set* an
appearance when Night was on; with it off, the application left the
decision to the Mac, so my Mac overrode a preference that plainly said
Day.

Appearance is three ways now: Day, Night, and Match system appearance.
It is applied at startup whatever it says, it is in Preferences and
under the View menu, and the old setting was migrated once so nobody's
choice was lost. Match system is the answer to the other half of my
question, whether night can arrive on its own: it follows the Mac,
sunset switch included.

## The missing labels under the icons were never missing

They were invisible. An application-wide stylesheet makes the toolkit
freeze each panel's colours the first time it draws them. Switching
appearance changed the colours but redrew nothing, so the labels kept
their daylight ink on a dark band. The fix had been written days ago and
never actually switched on.

The same defect is what made the shading look, in my own words, funky:
some panels dark, some half shaded, some not shaded at all. Twenty-five
places across eight panes had a manuscript colour written into the text
of the style itself, and a written-in colour cannot follow anything.
They are all computed from the palette now. Day is unchanged to the
pixel. Night is legible for the first time.

The worst of it was the Compare table. Its five row colours are measured
against cream paper, but behind a table row they sit on the window,
where the row's own text is nearly white --- a contrast of about 1.2 to
1, which is to say invisible. They keep their five meanings at a depth
the dark window can carry.

Two things fell out of that work. A damaged autosave in the Versions
list was painted warning red and then quiet grey one line later, so the
row that most needed to stand out was the only one that lost its colour.
And one of our colours clears the accessibility floor on paper at 4.62
but only reaches 4.22 on a light window, so its window sibling is a
shade darker. Three new tests hold all of it, including one that fails
the build outright if anyone writes a colour into a pane by hand again.

## The Sanskrit workbench

I pasted a verse from Nagarjuna and four of the six rows showed a
warning triangle with nothing beside it.

The refusal was correct in principle --- this engine never guesses ---
but it would not say what it could not read. It does now: the character,
its position, its code point, a plain statement that nothing was
guessed, and what to do next.

The cause was worse than the symptom. The converter knew only the five
ordinary whitespace characters, so a non-breaking space, which is
exactly what a word processor or a web page puts on the clipboard, made
it refuse an entire passage. A newline did the same. It reads every kind
of whitespace now, in both the reference engine and its working port
together.

A third fault surfaced while I was testing. Devanagari put its final
mark on the last word of a line and no other, so every word ending in a
consonant printed bare, with an implied vowel that is not in the
Sanskrit. The only Devanagari case in our test battery was the mani
mantra, and every word in it ends in a vowel --- which is precisely why
this survived. Both batteries now carry a word ending in a consonant.

Then I asked for more, and got it. The input is a paste area rather than
a single line. There are dropdowns for what you are converting from and
what you want it in. Every notation still gives the full analysis of a
single term, with Whitney's roots and the Mahavyutpatti bridge; choosing
one notation converts a whole passage line by line.

Here is the first verse of Nagarjuna's Root Verses on the Middle Way
going into Devanagari.

![The first verse of Nagarjuna's Root Verses on the Middle Way, IAST
into Devanagari. The bracketed label is carried through

Pasted documents are not all Sanskrit, so rules, headings and bracketed

## Mantras, read back from the Tibetan letters they are written in

This is the part I am most pleased with today, and it came from a single
observation: the mantras in the more esoteric texts are not Tibetan at
all. They are Sanskrit, written in Tibetan letters. So the tool should
take that script, or the Tibetanized ACIP of it, and give back an
accurate phonetic line for reciting.

That path existed only halfway, and every step of it was broken. Three
real faults, all found on actual mantras:

- The stack marker and the crescent mark of our Tibetan transliteration
  reached the Sanskrit intact, so the mani mantra met a converter that
  rightly refused them and came back with no pronunciation at all.
- The syllable spacing was being thrown away. For a single term that is
  right, because these notations separate syllables and not words. For a
  mantra it is exactly wrong: a mantra is recited syllable by syllable,
  and the spacing is the only guide to where they fall.
- Tibetan script was not an input option at all.

All three are fixed, in the reference engine and its working port
together. The mani mantra now reads back as **oːm mɐ ɳɪ pɐdmeː ɦuːm**,
syllable by syllable, and it is now one of our tests, with a control
asserting that no stack or crescent mark ever reaches the reader.

![Two mantras in Tibetan script, read back in every notation. The
pronunciation line names the authority it follows, and the simplified
line is labelled provisional.](img/2026-09-10/sanskrit-mantra.png)

On pronunciation I want to be exact about what we are standing on. The
IPA line follows the standard Classical Sanskrit mapping together with
FPMT Translation Services' guide to Sanskrit transliteration and
pronunciation of November 2020, with the rules for the nasal and the
final breath applied in context. That line is trustworthy. The
simplified line beneath it is not yet: it is an eighteen-rule table that
strips the diacritical marks, which gives a serviceable plain-letter
spelling and is not a pronunciation guide. It is labelled PROVISIONAL in
the interface for exactly that reason. Building a trustworthy simplified
respelling is a real piece of work and I would rather do it properly
than quietly.

## The Geshe Michael Roach Dictionary, as a spreadsheet

The dictionary drawn from the alignment of Geshe Michael Roach's courses
now exports as CSV every time it is rebuilt, from the same assembled
entries the web page renders, so the file and the page cannot disagree
about what the dictionary says.

Four files, each answering a different question.

- **The full export.** 20,933 rows over 10,758 headwords, courses one
  through five. One row per rendering, with wylie, ACIP, Tibetan script,
  pronunciation, the English, the depth, the number of occurrences, the
  courses, and the exact course and segment reference for every one.
  Each row also carries its own provenance --- the tier, the rule the
  English was gathered under, the corpus, the date --- because a
  spreadsheet gets forwarded and separated from its covering note, and
  this one will still say what it is.
- **English to Tibetan.** Whether Geshe Michael Roach has rendered a
  given word before, and as what. That is the question an editor
  actually asks, and the web page cannot answer it.
- **Each course's own glossary,** ranked by how often each term occurs
  in that course.
- **A change report** against the previous export. Nobody re-reads six
  thousand headwords. Everyone will read the delta.

Two findings came out of building it. The ACIP column has been empty for
every entry of the published page, because the index is stored
ACIP-to-wylie and was being read the other way round. And the layer
holds about fifteen headwords that are English course front matter
rather than Tibetan --- harmless as text, and fabrication the moment a
Tibetan script column is computed from them. So the two computed columns
are named for what they are, every row carries a note saying what
produced them, and a further column says whether the layer holds that
headword at all.

It is ready for Joel and waiting in my drafts.

## Decisions I made along the way

**Geshe Michael Roach's title goes with his name.** Everywhere: prose,
the interface, email, document titles and file names. It is the same
courtesy owed a doctor, and dropping it is not a shorthand. The
dictionary is the Geshe Michael Roach Dictionary; its four spreadsheets,
the web page, the page's own title and every reference in the tooling
were renamed this afternoon.

**Digests are centred from now on.** Section headers and every
screenshot with its caption. Body prose stays left-aligned, because a
centred paragraph is harder to read, not easier.

**His title goes with his name.** Everywhere --- prose, the interface,
email, document titles and file names. I also retired the internal
abbreviation we had been using for him, which stands for a form of
address only his close students use and is not how he should be referred
to publicly. Sixty-seven places in the application now say his name or a
plain three-letter label instead. What I deliberately did not rename are
the column names inside the data release itself; those belong to the
data project, and changing them here would break reading it.

**The ribbon's width promise held.** The idiom bank wanted a ribbon
button and would have pushed the ribbon past the width we promised
ourselves it would never exceed. It went to a right-click and a menu
instead.

## The Learn tab, and a phone

The afternoon turned into a long piece of work on the Drills, and then
into an iPhone app, because I kept finding the same fault from different
angles.

I sent seven screenshots of drills that looked wrong. Three separate
faults were behind them, and every one is now measured rather than
guessed.

**The English did not match the Tibetan in size.** The drill picks one
clause but printed my English for the whole segment. Measured over three
hundred real drills, the clause shown averaged **27.5%** of the segment
whose English sat above it --- a forty-word paragraph over three
syllables. It now shows the whole segment, with the answer blanked where
it actually stands.

**Raw source code was leaking into the Tibetan.** Options were rendering
as "(RNAMS", "(GNAS BRTAN)", "(ZHES". The converter was being handed
each word whole and, when it failed, printing the raw code inline as
though it were script. It hit 3.3% of drills.

**Title lines were being drilled as if they were sentences,** and far
more often than chance. This one was the real culprit: the adaptive draw
prefers short segments, and the title catalogue is the shortest material
we have --- ten to fifteen words against a corpus average near
thirty-six. Measured: **twenty to twenty-five percent of my drills were
catalogue entries,** and the title course was the single most-drawn of
all seventy-five. A quarter of everything I was seeing. All of it now
refused at the draw, along with 254 segments that hold English where the
Tibetan should be, which had been rendering as convincing nonsense.

I asked for titles to be labelled rather than hidden, and they are ---
the signal turned out to be structural rather than a guess about the
prose.

## The one I am most pleased to have caught

Late on I noticed an option beginning with an open parenthesis, against
a segment ending in a closing one. The punctuation was telling the
learner the answer before they read a word of Tibetan. Gone now: zero
options begin with a bracket.

And I said the English still often looked too long to be the equivalent.
It was, and the reason is exact. My C15 example measures **4.3 English
words per Tibetan word counting the bracketed material, and 1.4 without
--- 1.4 being the corpus median exactly.** The brackets are things I
supplied as translator: a restatement of the previous debate move, a
gloss, a page number. They have no Tibetan in that segment at all. Three
thousand segments carry some. They are now greyed and labelled as
supplied, rather than presented as the parallel.

## On the phone

The drills and the trainer now run as an iPhone app. The whole core
compiled for a real device unchanged --- seventy-seven files, no edits
--- which was the only genuine risk in the estimate. Everything is
generated on the Mac, so the phone inherits every refusal above and
carries no database at all.

![The Trainer on iPhone: read the passage yourself, then reveal one

Seeing it on a small screen immediately exposed something the desktop
had been hiding: one segment pairs **5,180 English words with five
Tibetan words**. The hint was longer than the lesson. There is now a
proportion ceiling, set from the measured distribution rather than by
eye.

It is going to **TestFlight** rather than the App Store. My decision, on
the recommendation that it reaches our team and the Mixed Nuts students

same upload, up to ten thousand testers.
