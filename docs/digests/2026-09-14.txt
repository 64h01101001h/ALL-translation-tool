# Diamond Cutter Translation Tool Daily Digest #7 --- Monday, September 14 2026

**Headline.** I glanced at the tool and saw it define a phrase from the
opening prayers as "farthest shore", which it does not mean. Underneath
that one wrong line was a rule the tool had been breaking in five
separate places: when it had a choice between Geshe Michael's own
English and a machine's guess, it was picking whichever covered more
words --- and the machine's guesses are longer. Everything below follows
from pulling that thread. The corrected tool is built, installed and
running.

## What got done

### The tool was preferring a machine's guess to his own words

The dictionary marks each English equivalent with where it came from. A
little over eight thousand are his, from his glossary and his own
curated list. About four thousand are a machine's attempt to match his
English to a Tibetan phrase, and those are labelled provisional because
that is what they are.

When the tool needed to show what a phrase means, it looked at every
possible reading and took **the longest one**. Nothing in that rule
asked which was his. So a four-word machine guess beat the two-word
curated entry sitting inside it, and "the Victors" --- his own word for
it, hand-curated --- was passed over for "farthest shore".

I measured how often. Of 877,004 times the reading page looks up a word,
**63,956 --- one in fourteen --- reached past his English to show a
machine's guess instead.** The translator's checking page was worse: it
*deleted* his term entirely rather than ranking it second, in **65% of
passages**.

It was written out by hand in five places and four of them had the same
fault. It is now one piece of code that all five use, with one test.

### Where the wrong definitions came from

The machine had to learn its guesses from somewhere, and I found it. Our
corpus pairs Tibetan with his English, and **1,066 of its 42,199
passages hold something in the English column that was never a
translation at all**:

- the **chanted prayers**, printed in transliteration so a student can
  chant along --- "sashi pukyi jukshing metok tram," is the *sound* of
  the Tibetan sitting where its meaning should be
- the **mantras**, Sanskrit carried over untranslated on purpose

Both belong in the course booklet. Neither is English, and the aligner
treated that column as ground truth. That is where "sadang lamgyi yunten
rabdzok" became a dictionary definition.

The tool now recognises both kinds. Sixty-four exercises on my phone
were presenting one of these as his English; there are none now. Where
the reading page quotes such a passage it still shows it --- it is what
the corpus says --- but it is labelled **NOT A TRANSLATION** rather than
badged as his.

### Three tests that had never tested anything

Chasing the first fault, I checked whether our automated tests would
have caught it. Several could not have:

- A test of whether his English is shown before reference material
  searched the page for two words that page never prints. It passed on
  their absence, every time, for months.
- A test marked in the code as **required** only ran if a randomly drawn
  flashcard happened to be a machine guess. On a fresh deck it ran not
  at all.
- A test proved the AI assistant receives his definitions, but never
  that it receives which ones are *his* --- the one thing that prompt
  calls mandatory.

All three now fail when they should. I check this by deliberately
breaking the thing each test guards and confirming the test notices; a
test I have not watched fail is a test I have not written.

### A correction, and it is mine

I published a figure yesterday saying 702 of our passages hold something
that is not English. **The right number is 1,066.** My measuring script
and the program disagreed about whether "a" and "I" count as English
words. The program was always right; my figure described something the
program does not do, and I had written it into a document and five
places in the code.

Corrected everywhere, with the cause recorded beside it. It is the same
fault I spent the day finding in the tool: a number that was never
checked against the thing it claimed to describe.

## Open questions for leadership

- **The corpus's English column.** The 1,066 passages are not mistakes
  in the source --- the transliteration genuinely belongs beside the
  Tibetan in a chanting booklet. The mistake was filing them under
  "English" and then teaching a machine from that column. Marking them
  once, at the point the material is ingested, would be exact where
  every downstream check is a guess. That is a decision about the
  dictionary project, not this app.
