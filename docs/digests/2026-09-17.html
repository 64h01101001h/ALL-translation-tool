Diamond Cutter Translation Tool Daily Digest #9 — Thursday, September 17 2026

Covers Wednesday the 16th and Thursday the 17th. Two days in one digest: the 16th’s work went uncovered while the reading below was running.

Headline. I had two of Tony Duff’s grammar books read cover to cover against our software — 1,082 pages — and it found four real faults in the engines, three of which were the kind nobody can see: the program produced confident, plausible Tibetan and was wrong. All four are fixed, and the alignment of Course 5 has started again.

What got done

Reading the grammar books against our own program

I asked for Tony Duff’s Standard Tibetan Grammar, both volumes, to be read in full and every point in them checked against what our software actually does. Every page was read — not sampled — and every claim was re-checked by a second reader who was told to doubt it. That second pass threw out 177 claims, which is the number I trust the exercise by.

It confirmed 647 places where our program already does the right thing, and 41 where it does not. Four of those were faults and are fixed:

  • The mark that tells a reader a Sanskrit word is present had stopped being produced at all. It was still in our tables; a fix I made the day before had made it unreachable. That one was mine, from the previous day, and nothing in the entire battery of checks noticed.
  • A Sanskrit letter was being drawn as a character that does not exist in the alphabet. 1,267 dictionary entries.
  • A seed syllable used in prayer, bhrūṃ, was coming back without the mark over it, and the program reported success.
  • The seed mantra oṃ āḥ hūṃ would not render its middle syllable. The other two had always worked.

Three of those four were silent — the program did not flag anything, it simply produced something wrong that looked right. That is the finding behind the finding, and three new automatic checks now exist because of it.

The checks we built to catch silent faults had never run

Three new checks were written this week, each one because a fault had reached the program without anything noticing. Late today, trying to verify a single figure for this digest, I found that none of the three had ever run — not failed, not skipped, simply absent.

The cause is ordinary and worth knowing. Our test runner uses the list of checks recorded the last time the project was set up, and that was two days earlier; every check written since was invisible to it. The same staleness meant the checks that did run were examining a two-day-old copy of the program, so three of them reported faults that were not real — the old program disagreeing with the corrected reference.

All three of the missing checks pass. Nothing was hiding behind them. But for two days this digest’s own figure, “automatic checks, all passing”, counted checks that had never executed, and no one could have produced that number from the project itself.

There is a check for this now. It refuses to proceed if the build folder is older than the files describing it, or if a check exists that nothing runs.

The engines are fixed; the dictionary they wrote still holds the old answers

Worth being plain about, because it is visible on screen today. Fixing an engine does not correct what that engine wrote earlier. The spellings in our dictionary were generated once and stored, so the corrections described above reach anything rendered from now on and reach nothing already saved.

Measured against all 105,634 entries: 97,432 already agree with the corrected engine, and 1,946 do not. They sort into three piles, and the sorting is the useful part:

  • 627 are safe to correct mechanically, 38 of them Geshe Michael’s own entries. These are the words where the old engine could not read the spelling and gave up, leaving a typewriter hyphen sitting inside the Tibetan — ལོག་ག-ཡེམ rather than ལོག་གཡེམ. That one is sexual misconduct, which is core vocabulary in this very course. Five of them are in the phone’s current practice pack.
  • 564 must be left alone. They look wrong to a machine and are not: the stored value is a headword followed by its variants, and a blanket rebuild would delete the variants.
  • 755 need a person, 47 of them Geshe Michael’s own. Neither side can be trusted blind — sometimes the stored spelling is right and the engine’s reading of it is wrong.

This is not ours to fix, and we have not touched it. The tool imports the dictionary; it does not own it. What we can hand over is the work order, and that is written: every entry listed, with its current value, the corrected value, and which of the three piles it belongs in. It regenerates from any version of the dictionary, so it does not go stale while it waits.

Thirty-seven things are not faults, and they are now five questions

The other 37 are places where Duff describes Tibetan one way and our software models it another. Nothing is broken; something is unmodelled, and whether to model it is a decision rather than a repair. Left as a list of 37 they read as a mess. They are not: they collapse into five questions, and answering those five closes all of them. That brief is written and waiting for you.

Three of Duff’s rules were measured and deliberately NOT implemented, because doing what the book says would have made the tool worse. One example: enforcing the classical rule about which letters may end a word would have made the program refuse 175 correct entries — Sanskrit written in Tibetan letters, where the rule does not apply. The measurements are recorded so the question is not re-opened without new evidence.

The first step for a beginner, measured at last

On a hike in September I asked for the one thing a beginner needs first: being able to tell where one word stops and the next begins. That work had been blocked since, because two honest attempts to count word frequency had disagreed by two and a half times, and a rule was set that no figure could be published until the disagreement was settled.

It is settled, and the answer is measured:

  • The most common 2,000 words cover 76% of everything we hold. The most common 4,000 cover 86%.
  • Thirty-two connecting particles alone are a fifth of all running text. Those are exactly the small words that show a reader where one word ends — the cheapest thing on the list, and the first thing to teach.
  • The choice that was feared would move the answer moves it by three or four points, not by two and a half times.

I published the first version of these figures and they were wrong by three points. Two spellings of the same word were being counted separately, and it was visible on the first line of output. The page now carries the wrong numbers and the reason beside the right ones.

Course 5 alignment resumed

The full-depth reading of Course 5 stopped on 8 September at segment 165 of 511, at my call, while the app went toward release. It has started again. Segment 180 of 511 now, five pages done to the full standard: two independent analysts on each sentence, their disagreements settled by a third against the evidence already banked.

On one sentence the two analysts agreed on only 25 of 44 alignments, which is what the two-reader method is for. Several proposed alignments were dropped rather than banked — each would have passed every automatic check and still been false. On one sentence alone there were four.

The layer has also become old enough to answer questions about itself. Two analysts disagreed over how deep one word sat; the reconciler found the same clause already read and banked eight sentences earlier, and let that settle it rather than argue.

A defect in Geshe Michael’s dictionary, found by reading the screen

I noticed the program showing the word gus — reverence — as “i”. It is not the program’s doing: the dictionary itself stores “i” first for that word. Measured: of 12,004 entries, ten lead with a single letter, and eight of the ten are correct — “i” is the pronoun I stored in lower case, and several Tibetan particles genuinely correspond to English endings. Two are wrong, gus among them.

That question also produced a tool: a way to narrow 11,968 entries to 55 worth a person’s eye. Reading those 55 found ten glosses that do not describe their headword, each confirmed against two independent dictionaries.

Adam’s decisions and actions

  • Ruled that Tony Duff’s dictionaries are to be trusted above Hopkins, on reliability and on register — Hopkins’s scholarly English is not the voice this project wants. Recorded, with two things it does not change: Geshe Michael’s own English stays binding above every reference work, and trust is not a licence — the Illuminator is asked for, not yet obtained.
  • Paused release work and directed that the alignment and the dictionary are the focus.
  • Found the gus defect by using the program.
  • Asked for the grammar books to be searched for on the drives. They were on the laptop the whole time, filed under “Tony Bluff” — a typo in the file name, which is why every search for the author had failed.

Open questions for leadership

  • The five questions in the decision brief. The largest: whether we should model the traditional Tibetan account of letter gender, from which the classical grammarians derive tense and other things. We currently use a Western description instead. It is not wrong; it is a different axis.
  • Whether “particle” is the right word. Duff argues it mistranslates the Tibetan, which means “phrase linker”. My own advice is to find out what Geshe Michael’s courses say first: if they say particle, the students’ vocabulary should win.
  • One reading needs a person who knows Sanskrit. Our software and the Illuminator disagree about a cluster of letters, and our own dictionary has nothing to say — not one of its 105,634 entries uses it.
  • Who applies the dictionary work order, and when. 627 mechanical corrections and 755 that need a reader are sitting ready. The corrections cannot land here — the tool imports the dictionary rather than owning it — so this needs whoever cuts the next dictionary release. Until then the wrong spellings stay on screen, on the desktop and on the phone.
  • What “all of Geshe Michael’s translations” means. The 42,199 aligned passages are a portion. Establishing what is missing needs the organisation’s records, not the program’s.
