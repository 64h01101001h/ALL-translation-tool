AI NOTE — Two kinds of karma, one sentence apart. The eye-patterns (mdongs) of a peacock come from las thun mong ma yin pa, karma that is NOT held in common — Geshe Michael's "each one's personal karma"; the petals and colors of a lotus come from thun mong gi las, karma that IS held in common — "the collective karma of living beings." That single pair of words carries the whole distinction, so both halves are wrapped: thun mong → "collective", thun mong ma yin pas → "personal" (the instrumental -s stays inside the span). Left unwrapped on purpose: "each one's", which compresses the whole run sems can de rnams kho na'i — an independent rendering of this same Tibetan elsewhere in the corpus reads "the unique karma only of living beings themselves", which is exactly the reading that will not compress onto any one word; "flower" after "lotus" and "cases" after "other", both supplied English nouns; and the whole closing frame "You can apply these principles", which recasts the gerundive shes par bya'o, "it is to be understood." A root verse from the Treasure House of Knowledge quoted inside Gendun Drup's commentary, followed by the commentary's own remark about the Mind-Only School: the outer world arises from the shared karma of living beings, and even the Mind-Only books discuss the question, so that school does not do away with the vessel world either.

Verse line four is flagged. The English takes the hells, the heavens above and the world as further items of the simile. The quoted sutra line is not banked. The Tibetan is the standard pair byed pa po (the one who acts) and tshor ba po (the one who experiences), but Geshe Michael's English gives two agent-words, “no great master of all things, and no great maker of all things”, so no experiencer is present. The second byed pa po, twenty syllables later, is unambiguous and is banked as “creator”.
