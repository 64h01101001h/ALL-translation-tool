AI NOTE — RECONCILED. The line closes the glass-of-liquid argument: how a thing stands dang por — originally — is settled by which snod kyi 'jig rten, which outer world, it was drawn from, and each of the three kinds of being has its own such world because each has its own thun mong ma yin pa'i las, its unshared karma. Geshe Michael restructures heavily, so several Tibetan words have no exponent and are left unlinked rather than stretched onto a hinge he supplied: the second la brten nas (his "by"), both occurrences of de nyid du, the first grub pa, and the closing yin pa'i phyir. "sweet" is deliberately not linked. The Tibetan reads chu dvangs shing bsil ba, clear and cool water. Segment 269 — not every craving spirit sees pus and blood

GESHE MICHAEL'S ENGLISH (VERBATIM)
When we say that a craving spirit looks at a stream of water and sees pus and blood, by the way, we are only talking about some kinds of craving spirits, and not all of them. AI NOTE — The segment qualifies the standard karma example: the craving spirit who sees a river as pus and blood is only some craving spirits ('ga' zhig), not all (thams cad), because the obscuration (sgrib pa) comes in three places — outer (phyi'i), inner (nang gi), and in the food or drink itself (zas skom nyid). Geshe Michael expands all three; only the first, phyi'i → "in the world around them", is banked, on the shape of the banked phyi'i → "outside of us". nang gi is nulled: "that are parts of their bodies" explains what an inner obstacle is rather than rendering the word. sgrib pa can is cut as sgrib pa ("obstacles") plus can ("with") rather than as one compound, following the bank. "We are only talking about", "kinds of", the three distributive "some" of the list, and "that prevent them from relieving their craving" have no Tibetan under them and stay unwrapped. There is, moreover, an example we can use for how, when the three different types of beings with their three karmas look all together at a glass full of something wet and flowing, there start to be three different objects, each confirmed by a valid perception. Suppose there is a ball of red-hot steel; one piece of this ball provides the material cause, and the "mantra of steel" provides a contributing factor. Due to these two, a person who has used the mantra of steel on his hand can touch the ball, but he doesn't undergo any sensation of heat; instead, he feels some other sensation.
