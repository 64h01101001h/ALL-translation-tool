AI NOTE — The Tibetan is one run-on sentence ending in zhing: "whichever of these we ourselves [pursue] — turning back birth in the lower realms, or accomplishing freedom and the state of the All-Knowing — this turning back of the clinging to the appearances of this life is important at all three: beginning, end, middle." GMR renders two Tibetan verbs twice, and each is claimed once. bsgrub pa governs only thar pa dang thams cad mkhyen pa'i go 'pang, so it is claimed at "attaining"; the fronted "hope to accomplish" is his summary of the whole gang la'ang frame and is left unwrapped. gal che is claimed at "essential"; "we must learn to" is the same predicate anticipated a sentence early, and "absolutely" is his intensifier. The second 'di belongs to zlog pa 'di — "this [act of] stopping", the subject of gal che — and is claimed at the capitalised "This" that is the subject of "is absolutely essential"; the "this" of "this habit" modifies zhen pa and is left unwrapped, as is "habit of" (GMR's gloss on zhen pa, clinging). Three things left unclaimed on purpose: (1) gsum is not the "three" of "the three lower realms" — that number is supplied; gsum counts thog mtha' bar and is unrendered (compare ILL:126, where the same idiom is rendered "at all three stages: at the beginning, at the end, and in between"). (2) skye ba (birth) has no exponent: "keeping ourselves out of" drops the noun, and "ourselves" is a supplied reflexive, which is why zlog pa takes only the head verb "keeping". (3) The genitives 'i (x2) and gi are unrendered. The Tibetan lists the stages thog · mtha' · bar (beginning · end · middle), the ordinary Tibetan idiom; GMR re-sorts them into beginning · middle · end, which is why those three spans cross. The first dang surfaces as "or" because GMR recasts the conjunction as a disjunction; the second is the "and" of "freedom and the state". go 'pang is spelled 'pang for 'phang in the source and is quoted as it stands — see the errata.

Segment 447 — yang gonpa on the uncertainty of death

TIBETAN (WYLIE)
de yang rgyal ba yang dgon pas, ,tshe nges med la nam 'chi'i dus btab med, ,bdud 'chi bdag kho brel khom gyi byus mi rtsi, ,dus de ring 'byung bzhi dang rnam shes 'gyes gyur na, ,gdeng dang blo bde tshags su tshud dam soms
GESHE MICHAEL'S ENGLISH (VERBATIM)
Listen to these descriptions of suffering life, first from the lips of the Victor, Yang Gonpa: You can't be sure you will live, Nor fix the time you die; This monster, the Lord of Death, Has no interest To come at your convenience. The four elements of your physical body And your mind Could go today their separate ways; Think about it: Can you ever feel secure, Can you ever feel at peace?65de yang → ∅ (‘and moreover’; GMR replaces the connective with a supplied lead-in, ‘Listen to these descriptions… first’)la → ∅ (locative/topic; unrendered)nam → ∅ (‘when’; absorbed into GMR’s ‘the time you die’)'i → ∅ (genitive on 'chi; unrendered)kho → ∅ (resumptive ‘he’; carried by ‘This monster’, unrendered)gyi → ∅ (genitive; unrendered)byus → ∅ (‘scheme, reckoning’; absorbed into ‘Has no interest’)dus → ∅ (‘time’; the second dus, absorbed into ‘today’)gyur → ∅ (‘become’; auxiliary, no exponent — ‘Could go’ is GMR’s)na → ∅ (conditional ‘if’; ‘Could’ is a modal reflex, not an exponent)dang → ∅ (the second ‘and’; GMR splits the pair into two questions)tshags su tshud → ∅ (idiom ‘hold together, come out right’; GMR distributes it over both ‘Can you ever feel…’ questions, so no single span can claim it)dam → ∅ (interrogative; surfaces only as the two question marks)
AI NOTE — A prose lead-in of GMR's own making (Listen to these descriptions of suffering life, first from the lips of) frames four lines of verse by Yang Gonpa (yang dgon pa, 1213–1258), whom the text titles rgyal ba, “Victor.” Only de yang rgyal ba yang dgon pas stands behind that opening sentence, so the framing words are deliberately left unwrapped. The mid-sentence capitals (Nor, This, Has, To, And, Could, Think, Can) are verse line-initials, not sentence starts. Read Tibetan-first, the four lines are: (1) tshe nges med la nam 'chi'i dus btab med — ‘life is uncertain and no date is set for when you die’; GMR turns the two meds into two English negatives, “can't” and “Nor”. (2) bdud 'chi bdag kho brel khom gyi byus mi rtsi — literally ‘the demon, the Lord of Death, he does not reckon (rtsi) the scheme (byus) of your being busy or free (brel khom)’; GMR renders brel khom as “convenience”, mi rtsi as “no interest”, supplies “To come at”, and lets byus go unspoken. (3) dus de ring 'byung bzhi dang rnam shes 'gyes gyur na — ‘if today the four elements and the consciousness should part’; “of your physical body” is supplied (there is no lus), and dus is left unrendered because “today” already carries de ring. (4) gdeng dang blo bde tshags su tshud dam soms — ‘think whether assurance and ease of mind will hold together’; the imperative soms stands last in Tibetan but first in the English (“Think about it”), and the idiom tshags su tshud is spread across both “Can you ever feel…” questions rather than landing in one place, so it is left unclaimed.
